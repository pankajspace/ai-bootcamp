Section 10 · Part 1: the problem

Where one-shot retrieval breaks

10.1Naive RAG vs agentic RAG in one picture

You already know the RAG pipeline: split documents into chunks, turn each chunk into a vector, and at question time fetch the top-k most similar chunks and hand them to the LLM. We'll call that naive RAG. "Naive" isn't an insult: it's the right tool for simple lookups. The word just means it runs the same fixed steps for every question, with no decisions and no checking.

🧠 Analogy: the library assistant vs the research analyst

Naive RAG is a library assistant who hears your question, grabs the three books whose covers sound most like your words, reads you a paragraph, and leaves. Agentic RAG is a research analyst: they think "I need the company's latest figure and our internal limit", pull the right filing, notice the limit isn't in it, walk to the policy binder, and come back with an answer that cites both pages.

💰 Finance corner: the three documents and the quick ratio read this once

The quick ratio asks: if short-term bills all came due tomorrow, could the company pay them without selling inventory?

quick ratio = (current assets − inventories) ÷ current liabilities Acme, year-end 2024 = (498.0 − 119.8) ÷ 307.4 = 378.2 ÷ 307.4 = 1.23

Below 1.0 means the company needs to sell stock (inventory) or borrow to pay its short-term bills. The current ratio is the same thing with inventory (498.0 ÷ 307.4 = 1.62), so it is always the larger of the two.

⚠️ "Just raise k" is not a fix

At k = 3 the policy text crowds out the earnings call, because the question's words ("firm", "risk policy", "quick ratio") match the policy best. At k = 4 both facts arrive, but you can't pick k per question in advance. Raising k further adds the 10-K's 1.23, a competing number from a different period, plus more tokens and noise. The real fix is to decide where to look and check what came back. That's what the next three patterns do.

🎯 Interview angle: "Why not just use a bigger context window?"

Three reasons. Cost and latency grow with every token, on every question. Quality drops when the relevant sentence is buried in a long context. And it doesn't fix the logic: the model still has to know that the policy is a rule, the call is the latest figure and the 10-K is the controlling source. Long context and agentic retrieval work together: the agent keeps the context small and relevant.

Section 10 · Part 2: the three patterns

Three ways to give retrieval a brain

10.2Routing, decomposition, self-correction

All three patterns keep the same indexes. What changes is how much the LLM decides before and during retrieval. Think of it as a dial: more autonomy handles harder questions, but costs more LLM calls and is harder to test.

Routingpick ONE source
1 extra LLM call. For single-source questions over many documents.
Decompositionsplit, then fan out
A plan + one call per part. For questions with independent parts.
Self-correcting agentsearch, check, repeat
As many calls as it needs (capped). For open or dependent questions.

Pattern 1: Routing (pick the right shelf)

Keep one index per document (or per document type) instead of one blended index. Wrap each index as a tool with a name and a one-paragraph description. A selector LLM reads the question and the descriptions and picks the best tool. Only that index is searched.

🧠 Analogy: the bank's reception desk

A receptionist doesn't know mortgage law or FX hedging. They read the signs on the doors ("Mortgages", "Foreign exchange", "Compliance") and send you to the right one. If the signs are vague ("Department A", "Department B"), even a brilliant receptionist sends you to the wrong room. Tool descriptions are the signs on the doors.

In production, descriptions are often backed up by hard signals: metadata (doc_type="policy", period="Q3-2025"), keyword rules, or a small trained classifier.

Pattern 2: Query decomposition (split the question)

Routing still sends the whole question to one place, so it can't answer our two-document question. Decomposition asks an LLM to rewrite the question as sub-questions, each tagged with the tool that should answer it. Each sub-question runs as a normal RAG query on its own index, and a final LLM call combines the answers.

🧠 Analogy: the checklist handed to two interns

You write a checklist ("get Acme's Q3 quick ratio from the call transcript", "get our minimum from the policy binder"), give one line to each intern, and combine what they bring back. Fast and clear, as long as neither line depends on the other's answer.

⚠️ The limit: all sub-questions are written up front

The plan is made before any retrieval happens. So it can't handle dependent (multi-hop) questions, where step 2 needs step 1's answer: "Who is Acme's largest customer, and what is that customer's credit rating?" You can't write the second sub-question until you know the customer's name. For chains like this you need retrieval interleaved with reasoning: an agent (next), or research methods such as self-ask (the model asks itself follow-up questions) and IRCoT (retrieve again after every reasoning step).

Pattern 3: The self-correcting agent (search until the file is complete)

Give the same three tools to an agent with a clear goal: "for a compliance check, get the issuer's figure and the policy threshold; if something is missing, search again; if you can't find it, say so." Now the plan isn't fixed. After every tool call the agent looks at what it has and decides the next move.

Guardrails that make the loop safe

1Cite every figureSource document and reporting period ("1.18, Q3 FY2025 call"). Uncited numbers are a red flag in review.
2Cap the stepsmax_iterations stops a confused agent from searching forever. On the cap, write the best answer so far and say it's incomplete.
3Fail safeIf a required figure can't be found: "insufficient evidence", never an estimate. In compliance, a refusal is cheap; a wrong "yes" is expensive.
4Log the trajectoryRecord every tool call and its input. That's how you debug, and how you show an auditor where each number came from.
Research names you may hear in interviews optional
  • Self-RAG: a model trained to decide when to retrieve and to critique its own output with special reflection tokens.
  • CRAG (Corrective RAG): a light evaluator grades the retrieved documents; if they look poor, the system rewrites the query or searches elsewhere (e.g. the web) before answering.
  • FLARE: while writing, if the model is unsure about the next sentence, it pauses and retrieves first.

All three share the idea behind our agent: check retrieval quality, and retrieve again when it isn't good enough.

Section 10 · Part 3: better retrieval

Hybrid search and reranking

10.3Make every single search better

The three patterns decide where and how often to search. They can't help if each individual search returns the wrong chunks. Two upgrades fix that, and they work with any pattern: hybrid search casts a wider, smarter net (better recall), and reranking keeps only the best catch (better precision).

🧠 Analogy: two sniffer dogs

One dog follows the scent of meaning: it finds things that are about the same topic even if the words differ. The other follows exact footprints: it never confuses "Q3" with "Q2", but loses the trail if the words change. Send both, then combine their finds by a fair vote. That vote is reciprocal rank fusion.

BM25 in plain English

BM25 ("best matching, version 25") is the standard keyword-ranking formula, a refined version of the classic TF-IDF. For each word in the question it adds up a score built from three dials:

Term frequency (k1)more is better, up to a pointA chunk that says "quick ratio" twice beats one that says it once, but ten times isn't ten times better. k1 (≈1.2–2.0) sets how fast the bonus flattens out.
Rarity (idf)rare words count more"Acme" appears everywhere in our corpus, so it barely helps. "Quick" appears in a few chunks, so it's a strong signal.
Length (b)long ≠ relevantA long chunk contains more words by accident. b (≈0.75) scales scores down for longer-than-average chunks.

Here it is from scratch: about 20 lines, no libraries. It's part of rrf_by_hand.py, which runs free with no API key (full file in the appendix).

Python · rrf_by_hand.py · BM25 from scratch
import math
import re
from pathlib import Path

DATA_DIR = Path(__file__).parent / "data"


def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Lower-case words and numbers: 'Quick ratio was 1.18.' → ['quick','ratio','was','1.18']"""
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:\.[0-9]+)?", text.lower())


def bm25_scores(query: str, docs: list[str], k1: float = 1.5, b: float = 0.75) -> list[float]:
    """Score every doc for the query with BM25.

    For each query word:
        idf  = how RARE the word is across all docs   (rare words matter more)
        tf   = how often it appears in THIS doc       (more is better, but saturates)
        norm = penalty for long docs                  (long docs aren't better by default)
    score = Σ idf × tf×(k1+1) / (tf + k1×norm)
    """
    tokenized = [tokenize(d) for d in docs]
    n_docs = len(docs)
    avg_len = sum(len(t) for t in tokenized) / n_docs
    scores = []
    for words in tokenized:
        score = 0.0
        for term in set(tokenize(query)):
            n_with_term = sum(1 for t in tokenized if term in t)
            if n_with_term == 0:
                continue
            idf = math.log(1 + (n_docs - n_with_term + 0.5) / (n_with_term + 0.5))
            tf = words.count(term)
            norm = 1 - b + b * len(words) / avg_len           # = 1 for an average-length doc
            score += idf * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 * norm)
        scores.append(score)
    return scores
  1. tokenize turns "Quick ratio was 1.18." into ['quick', 'ratio', 'was', '1.18']. The regex keeps decimal numbers whole, so 1.18 is one "word". That matters in finance.
  2. avg_len is the average chunk length in words; it's the yardstick for the length dial.
  3. For each chunk, for each distinct query word: n_with_term counts the chunks that contain the word, and idf is large when that count is small (a rare word).
  4. tf is how often the word appears in this chunk. The fraction tf × (k1+1) / (tf + k1 × norm) grows with tf but levels off. Try it: with k1 = 1.5 and an average-length chunk, tf = 1 gives 1.0, tf = 2 gives 1.43, tf = 10 gives 2.17.
  5. norm is 1.0 for an average-length chunk, above 1 for a longer one (which lowers the score) and below 1 for a shorter one.

Fusing two rankings: Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

Now you have two ranked lists. You can't just add the scores: cosine similarities sit around 0.3–0.8, while BM25 scores are unbounded (7.54 in our run). Adding them lets BM25 drown out meaning search. RRF ignores the scores completely and uses only positions:

RRF score(chunk) = Σ over each list that contains it of 1 / (k + rank) rank starts at 0, k = 60 1st place in one list → 1/60 = 0.0167 2nd in one list + 3rd in other → 1/61 + 1/62 = 0.0325 (appearing in BOTH beats topping one)

The constant k = 60 comes from the original RRF paper and is LlamaIndex's default. It makes neighbouring ranks almost equal (1/60 vs 1/61), so agreement between the two searches matters more than who came first. A smaller k gives the top spots more weight.

Python · rrf_by_hand.py · rank_ids and rrf
def rank_ids(scores: list[float]) -> list[int]:
    """Doc ids ordered best → worst (only docs with a positive score)."""
    return [i for i in sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: -scores[i]) if scores[i] > 0]


def rrf(ranked_lists: list[list[int]], k: int = 60) -> dict[int, float]:
    """Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Each list gives a doc 1/(k + rank), rank starting at 0.

    Raw scores are ignored completely — only POSITIONS count. So a BM25 score of 9.7
    and a cosine of 0.41 can be merged fairly. Appearing in BOTH lists beats topping one.
    """
    fused: dict[int, float] = {}
    for ranking in ranked_lists:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking):
            fused[doc_id] = fused.get(doc_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return dict(sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]))
  1. rank_ids sorts chunk ids by score, best first, and drops zero scores (chunks that share no words with the query).
  2. rrf walks each ranked list. enumerate gives the position (0, 1, 2…) and the chunk id; the chunk's running total grows by 1 / (k + rank). A chunk in both lists collects two contributions.
  3. The last line sorts the totals, highest first: that's the fused ranking.

💰 Finance corner: when search should walk relationships (Graph RAG)

Some questions are about connections, not passages: "Which of our holdings depend on the same actuator supplier as Acme?" or "Which counterparties are exposed to Acme's largest customer?" Graph RAG first extracts entities (companies, suppliers, customers) and relationships into a knowledge graph, then answers by following edges. It's powerful for supply-chain and counterparty questions, but expensive to build and keep current. Reach for it when relationship questions are common, not by default.

Reranking: a wide net, then a careful pick

A keyword screen of 5,000 CVs is fast and finds everyone plausible (recall). Then a senior banker interviews the top 20 properly and keeps 3 (precision). You'd never interview all 5,000, and you'd never hire straight from the keyword screen.

Section 10 · Part 4: measurement

How do you know the answer can be trusted?

10.4The RAG triad: three checks, three failure modes

A RAG answer touches three things: the question, the retrieved context, and the generated answer. Check each pair and you get the three standard metrics. Each one points at a different broken part, which is why you need all three.

🧠 Analogy: three questions an editor asks a junior analyst

"Did you pull the right sources?" (context relevancy) · "Can you show me where every sentence comes from?" (faithfulness) · "Did you actually answer the client's question?" (answer relevancy). A report can pass two and fail the third, and each failure has a different fix.

Faithfulness, step by step

Faithfulness catches hallucination: claims the context doesn't support. A judge LLM (1) splits the answer into separate claims, (2) checks each claim against the retrieved chunks, and (3) scores supported claims ÷ total claims. Note that "true in the real world" isn't enough: the claim must be supported by the retrieved context.

💰 Why finance demands 1.0 faithfulness

In a chatbot, 0.9 faithfulness sounds great. In a compliance memo, it means one claim in ten has no source, and you don't know which one. Production finance systems typically require every figure to be supported (faithfulness = 1.0, or a hard per-claim check) and block or escalate anything else. Derived numbers (like a growth rate) should either be computed by a tool, so the calculation is on record, or shown with their inputs.

def show(title: str, question: str, answer, nodes=None) -> None:

from llama_index.core.agent.workflow import FunctionAgent, ToolCallResult

AGENT_SYSTEM_PROMPT = (
    "You are a buy-side compliance analyst. Answer using ONLY the document tools "
    "provided. First work out what evidence the question needs: a compliance check "
    "usually needs BOTH the issuer's figure from its filings AND the matching "
    "threshold from the firm's risk policy. Before concluding, check that you have "
    "every piece; if one tool call did not give you everything, call another tool. "
    "If a required figure cannot be found, say so explicitly instead of estimating. "
    "Cite the source document and reporting period for every figure you use."
)
MAX_AGENT_STEPS = 8          # guardrail: never loop forever, however confused the model is


def run_agent_sync(agent: FunctionAgent, task: str):
    """Run the agent; also record its tool calls (the 'trajectory') and evidence chunks."""
    trajectory: list[str] = []
    evidence: list = []

    async def _run():
        handler = agent.run(task, max_iterations=MAX_AGENT_STEPS,
                            early_stopping_method="generate")
        async for event in handler.stream_events():          # watch it work, live
            if isinstance(event, ToolCallResult):
                trajectory.append(f"{event.tool_name}({event.tool_kwargs.get('input', '')!r})")
                raw = getattr(event.tool_output, "raw_output", None)   # the tool's Response
                evidence.extend(getattr(raw, "source_nodes", None) or [])
        return await handler

    response = asyncio.run(_run())
    return response, trajectory, evidence


def step_agent(indices: dict[str, VectorStoreIndex]) -> None:
    agent = FunctionAgent(
        tools=build_query_engine_tools(indices),
        llm=Settings.llm,
        system_prompt=AGENT_SYSTEM_PROMPT,
    )
    task = ("Can we open a new long position in Acme Robotics under the firm's liquidity "
            "policy? Check the quick-ratio rule against Acme's most recent disclosed "
            "quick ratio.")
    for q, keep in ((task, False), (COMPARISON_QUESTION, True)):
        response, trajectory, evidence = run_agent_sync(agent, q)
        print("\n  agent tool calls, in order:")
        for i, call in enumerate(trajectory, 1):
            print(f"   {i}. {call}")
        show("SELF-CORRECTING AGENT", q, response, evidence)
        if keep:
            COMPARISON["agent"] = str(response)
  1. AGENT_SYSTEM_PROMPT is the investigation manual: work out what evidence is needed (figure and threshold), check before concluding, call another tool if something's missing, never estimate, cite document and period. The loop's behaviour comes from these sentences.
  2. agent.run(task, max_iterations=8, early_stopping_method="generate") starts the run and returns a handler straight away; the agent works in the background. The step cap is a guardrail; "generate" means "at the cap, write the best answer from what you have" instead of raising an error.
  3. async for event in handler.stream_events() lets us watch it work. Each ToolCallResult event carries tool_name (which document), tool_kwargs["input"] (the search the agent wrote, e.g. "Acme Q3 quick ratio"), and tool_output.raw_output, the tool's full Response, whose source_nodes are the chunks. We append those to trajectory and evidence.
  4. await handler waits for the final answer. asyncio.run(_run()) runs the async code from ordinary synchronous Python.
  5. The printed trajectory is the proof of self-correction: typically firm_risk_policy(...) first, then q3_earnings_call(...) once the agent notices it has the rule but not the figure.

from llama_index.core.query_engine import RetrieverQueryEngine
from llama_index.core.retrievers import QueryFusionRetriever
from llama_index.retrievers.bm25 import BM25Retriever


def build_hybrid_retriever(combined: VectorStoreIndex) -> QueryFusionRetriever:
    """Meaning search + keyword search over the SAME chunks, merged by rank (RRF)."""
    dense = combined.as_retriever(similarity_top_k=5)                  # by meaning
    sparse = BM25Retriever.from_defaults(docstore=combined.docstore,   # by exact words
                                         similarity_top_k=5)
    return QueryFusionRetriever(
        [dense, sparse],
        similarity_top_k=5,          # keep the best 5 after fusion
        num_queries=1,               # 1 = no LLM rewrites of the question (cheap, repeatable)
        mode="reciprocal_rerank",    # Reciprocal Rank Fusion: score = Σ 1/(60 + rank)
        use_async=False,
    )


def step_hybrid(combined: VectorStoreIndex) -> None:
    engine = RetrieverQueryEngine.from_args(build_hybrid_retriever(combined))
    answer = engine.query(COMPARISON_QUESTION)
    show("HYBRID (dense + BM25, fused)", COMPARISON_QUESTION, answer)
    COMPARISON["hybrid"] = str(answer)
  1. combined.as_retriever(similarity_top_k=5) is the dense side. A retriever only fetches chunks; a query engine fetches and then writes an answer.
  2. BM25Retriever.from_defaults(docstore=combined.docstore) builds a keyword index over the same chunks; the docstore holds every chunk's text. No embeddings, no API calls.
  3. QueryFusionRetriever runs both, fuses with RRF (mode="reciprocal_rerank", k = 60), and keeps the top 5. num_queries=1 means no LLM-written paraphrases.
  4. RetrieverQueryEngine.from_args(retriever) adds the answer-writing step on top. On a corpus this tiny the fused scores look alike (≈0.016 vs ≈0.033); on thousands of chunks the differences matter much more.

    AnswerRelevancyEvaluator,
    ContextRelevancyEvaluator,
    FaithfulnessEvaluator,
)


def step_evaluate(indices: dict[str, VectorStoreIndex]) -> dict:
    engine = indices["ten_k"].as_query_engine(similarity_top_k=3)
    q = "What was Acme's net income per diluted share in fiscal 2024?"
    response = engine.query(q)
    show("EVALUATE — the answer being graded", q, response)

    # The RAG triad. Each uses the LLM as a judge with a fixed grading prompt.
    faithful = FaithfulnessEvaluator().evaluate_response(response=response)
    answer_rel = AnswerRelevancyEvaluator().evaluate_response(query=q, response=response)
    context_rel = ContextRelevancyEvaluator().evaluate_response(query=q, response=response)

    scores = {
        "faithful": faithful.passing,          # True/False: every claim backed by chunks?
        "answer_relevancy": answer_rel.score,  # 0–1: does the answer address the question?
        "context_relevancy": context_rel.score,  # 0–1: were the fetched chunks on topic?
    }
    print("\n  RAG triad:", scores)
    return scores
  1. Each evaluator reads response.source_nodes as the context automatically, so you only pass the response (and the question, for the two relevancy metrics).
  2. Each evaluate_response is one or more judge calls to Settings.llm. In production you'd often use a different model as the judge, to avoid a model grading its own homework.
  3. For this simple question expect faithful=True and high relevancy. The interesting cases are the failures: run the evaluators on the router's comparison answer and see faithfulness or relevancy drop.

    main(wanted)

  2. main builds the indexes once, runs the chosen steps, then prints every pattern's answer to the comparison question, side by side.
  3. A mistyped step name (python agentic_rag_lab.py routr) exits with the list of valid names before any API call is made.

Interview prep

12 questions you can now answer well

Click a question, answer it out loud first, then compare. Good answers are short, give a reason, and name a trade-off.

What is agentic RAG, and how is it different from naive RAG?

Naive RAG runs a fixed pipeline: embed the question, fetch the top-k chunks, generate once. Agentic RAG puts an LLM in control of retrieval: it chooses which source to search, can split or rephrase the question, checks whether the evidence is enough, and searches again if it isn't. It handles multi-source and multi-step questions, at the cost of more LLM calls and less predictable latency.

Router, sub-question decomposition, or agent: how do you choose?

Use the least autonomy that answers the question. One right source → router. Independent parts across sources → decomposition. Dependent steps, or answers that must be checked before they're given → an agent with a step cap and citation rules. In production, a cheap router often sends easy questions to plain RAG and wakes the agent only for hard ones.

Why do tool descriptions matter so much, and how do you validate routing?

The selector never sees the documents, only the names and descriptions, so vague or overlapping descriptions send questions to the wrong source. Each description should say what's unique about its source (period, audited or not, rules vs facts). Validate it like a classifier: a labelled set of question → correct tool, re-measured whenever descriptions or documents change, with the selector's choice and reason logged for every query. Back it up with metadata or rules where they are more reliable.

What can't SubQuestionQueryEngine do?

It writes all sub-questions before retrieving anything, so it can't handle dependent multi-hop questions where step 2 needs step 1's answer ("find the largest customer, then that customer's credit rating"). For those, interleave retrieval and reasoning: an agent, or methods like self-ask and IRCoT. It also adds about one LLM call per sub-question plus the plan and the final combine.

How do you stop an agentic RAG loop from running forever or making things up?

A hard step cap (max_iterations) with a defined behaviour at the cap; a system prompt that requires a citation (document and period) for every figure; an explicit fail-safe ("insufficient evidence", never an estimate); a token or cost budget; and trajectory logging so every tool call can be reviewed. A faithfulness check on the final answer adds a second line of defence.

Dense vs sparse retrieval: why use both in finance?

Dense (embedding) search matches meaning but is weak on exact tokens such as tickers, bond names, section numbers and specific figures. Sparse BM25 matches exact words and rewards rare ones, but misses synonyms ("acid-test" vs "quick ratio"). Finance text is full of both identifiers and paraphrase, so hybrid search improves recall. BM25 is also cheap: no API, just word statistics.

Explain reciprocal rank fusion. Why ranks and not scores?

Each chunk gets Σ 1/(k + rank) over the lists it appears in, with rank starting at 0 and k = 60 by convention. Scores from different retrievers aren't comparable (cosine ≈ 0–1, BM25 unbounded), so adding them lets one retriever dominate. Using ranks is scale-free, and with k = 60 neighbouring ranks are worth almost the same, so a chunk found by both retrievers (≈0.033) beats one that tops a single list (≈0.017).

Bi-encoder, cross-encoder, LLM reranker: what's the difference?

A bi-encoder embeds the query and chunk separately and compares vectors: very fast, used for first-stage retrieval. A cross-encoder reads query and chunk together and outputs one score: more accurate, fast enough to rerank tens of chunks locally. An LLM reranker such as LLMRerank ranks a batch of chunks in one prompt (listwise, 1–10 scores): most flexible, but one LLM call per batch. Typical stack: hybrid retrieval for ~20–50 candidates, then a reranker down to 3–5.

Explain the RAG triad and how you'd use it to debug.

Context relevancy (question ↔ context) judges the retriever. Faithfulness (context ↔ answer) judges whether the generator stuck to the evidence. Answer relevancy (question ↔ answer) judges whether the question was actually answered. Low context relevancy → fix retrieval (routing, hybrid, chunking, reranking). Good context but low faithfulness → the generator is hallucinating (prompt, citations, model). Faithful but low answer relevancy → it answered a different question (query understanding, decomposition).

Faithfulness is 0.9. Is that good enough for a compliance answer?

No. It means roughly one claim in ten has no support in the retrieved evidence, and you don't know which. For compliance and client-facing finance, require every figure to be supported (1.0, or a per-claim check) and block or escalate otherwise. Derived numbers should be computed by a tool so the calculation is on record. And remember the judge itself is a model: pin its version and calibrate it against human grades.

Two documents give different numbers for the same metric. What should the system do?

Never pick one silently. Apply a written source hierarchy (e.g. the audited 10-K controls, quarterly figures are monitored for deterioration), report both values with their periods, apply the rule to the controlling figure, flag the change, and escalate if the latest figure breaches a limit. Put the hierarchy in the agent's instructions and in metadata, and keep a regression test where sources disagree.

How would you cut the cost and latency of an agentic RAG system?

Route simple questions to plain RAG; cap agent steps; run sub-questions concurrently; use a cross-encoder instead of an LLM reranker; keep query rewriting off (num_queries=1) unless recall needs it; use metadata filters so searches are smaller; cache embeddings and frequent answers; and use a smaller model for selection and reranking, keeping the strongest model for the final synthesis.

def run_agent_sync(agent: FunctionAgent, task: str):
    """Run the agent; also record its tool calls (the 'trajectory') and evidence chunks."""
    trajectory: list[str] = []
    evidence: list = []

    async def _run():
        handler = agent.run(task, max_iterations=MAX_AGENT_STEPS,
                            early_stopping_method="generate")
        async for event in handler.stream_events():          # watch it work, live
            if isinstance(event, ToolCallResult):
                trajectory.append(f"{event.tool_name}({event.tool_kwargs.get('input', '')!r})")
                raw = getattr(event.tool_output, "raw_output", None)   # the tool's Response
                evidence.extend(getattr(raw, "source_nodes", None) or [])
        return await handler

    response = asyncio.run(_run())
    return response, trajectory, evidence

def build_hybrid_retriever(combined: VectorStoreIndex) -> QueryFusionRetriever:
    """Meaning search + keyword search over the SAME chunks, merged by rank (RRF)."""
    dense = combined.as_retriever(similarity_top_k=5)                  # by meaning
    sparse = BM25Retriever.from_defaults(docstore=combined.docstore,   # by exact words
                                         similarity_top_k=5)
    return QueryFusionRetriever(
        [dense, sparse],
        similarity_top_k=5,          # keep the best 5 after fusion
        num_queries=1,               # 1 = no LLM rewrites of the question (cheap, repeatable)
        mode="reciprocal_rerank",    # Reciprocal Rank Fusion: score = Σ 1/(60 + rank)
        use_async=False,
    )

    answer = engine.query(COMPARISON_QUESTION)

    COMPARISON["hybrid"] = str(answer)

"""
Section 10 — BM25 and Reciprocal Rank Fusion from scratch (no API key, no libraries)
===================================================================================
Hybrid search = two ranked lists (meaning search + keyword search) merged into one.
This script builds the keyword side (BM25) and the merge (RRF) by hand, so you can
see every number. It runs instantly and costs nothing.

How to run
----------
    python rrf_by_hand.py

It reads the three documents in ./data, splits them into paragraphs, ranks them with
BM25 for the compliance question, fuses that list with a dense (meaning) ranking, and
shows why we fuse RANKS instead of adding raw SCORES.
"""

import math
import re
from pathlib import Path

DATA_DIR = Path(__file__).parent / "data"


def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    """Lower-case words and numbers: 'Quick ratio was 1.18.' → ['quick','ratio','was','1.18']"""
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:\.[0-9]+)?", text.lower())


def bm25_scores(query: str, docs: list[str], k1: float = 1.5, b: float = 0.75) -> list[float]:
    """Score every doc for the query with BM25.

    For each query word:
        idf  = how RARE the word is across all docs   (rare words matter more)
        tf   = how often it appears in THIS doc       (more is better, but saturates)
        norm = penalty for long docs                  (long docs aren't better by default)
    score = Σ idf × tf×(k1+1) / (tf + k1×norm)
    """
    tokenized = [tokenize(d) for d in docs]
    n_docs = len(docs)
    avg_len = sum(len(t) for t in tokenized) / n_docs
    scores = []
    for words in tokenized:
        score = 0.0
        for term in set(tokenize(query)):
            n_with_term = sum(1 for t in tokenized if term in t)
            if n_with_term == 0:
                continue
            idf = math.log(1 + (n_docs - n_with_term + 0.5) / (n_with_term + 0.5))
            tf = words.count(term)
            norm = 1 - b + b * len(words) / avg_len           # = 1 for an average-length doc
            score += idf * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 * norm)
        scores.append(score)
    return scores

def rank_ids(scores: list[float]) -> list[int]:
    """Doc ids ordered best → worst (only docs with a positive score)."""
    return [i for i in sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: -scores[i]) if scores[i] > 0]


def rrf(ranked_lists: list[list[int]], k: int = 60) -> dict[int, float]:
    """Reciprocal Rank Fusion. Each list gives a doc 1/(k + rank), rank starting at 0.

    Raw scores are ignored completely — only POSITIONS count. So a BM25 score of 9.7
    and a cosine of 0.41 can be merged fairly. Appearing in BOTH lists beats topping one.
    """
    fused: dict[int, float] = {}
    for ranking in ranked_lists:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking):
            fused[doc_id] = fused.get(doc_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return dict(sorted(fused.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]))

def load_paragraphs() -> list[tuple[str, str]]:
    """(file name, paragraph) for every non-heading paragraph in ./data."""
    out = []
    for path in sorted(DATA_DIR.glob("*.md")):
        for para in path.read_text(encoding="utf-8").split("\n\n"):
            if para.strip() and not para.startswith("#"):
                out.append((path.name, " ".join(para.split())))
    return out


def find(paras, needle: str) -> int:
    return next(i for i, (_, p) in enumerate(paras) if needle in p)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    paras = load_paragraphs()
    texts = [p for _, p in paras]
    question = "Does Acme's Q3 quick ratio satisfy the firm's risk policy?"

    # 1) Keyword side: BM25 over every paragraph
    bm25 = bm25_scores(question, texts)
    bm25_rank = rank_ids(bm25)[:5]

    # 2) Meaning side: in the full lab this list comes from the vector index.

    #    embeddings match MEANING, so the paragraph about "liquidity" and "inventory
    #    build" ranks high even though it never says "risk policy".
    dense_rank = [find(paras, "quick ratio at quarter-end"),
                  find(paras, "2.1 Quick ratio"),
                  find(paras, "Should investors worry about liquidity"),
                  find(paras, "2.4 Source of figures"),
                  find(paras, "Excluding inventories")]

    print(f"Question: {question}\n")
    print("BM25 top 5 (keyword match):")
    for r, i in enumerate(bm25_rank):
        print(f"  rank {r}  score {bm25[i]:5.2f}  {paras[i][0]:<26} {texts[i][:55]}…")
    print("\nDense top 5 (meaning match):")
    for r, i in enumerate(dense_rank):
        print(f"  rank {r}               {paras[i][0]:<26} {texts[i][:55]}…")

    # 3) Fuse by rank
    fused = rrf([dense_rank, bm25_rank])
    print("\nRRF fused (k=60):  score = Σ 1/(60 + rank)")
    for i, s in list(fused.items())[:5]:
        where = [name for name, lst in (("dense", dense_rank), ("bm25", bm25_rank)) if i in lst]
        ranks = ", ".join(f"{n}#{(dense_rank if n == 'dense' else bm25_rank).index(i)}" for n in where)
        print(f"  {s:.4f}  [{ranks:<16}] {paras[i][0]:<26} {texts[i][:45]}…")

    # 4) Why not just add the raw scores?
    top_bm25 = bm25[bm25_rank[0]]
    print(f"\nWhy fuse ranks, not scores: a top BM25 score here is {top_bm25:.2f}, while a top")
    print("cosine similarity is usually 0.3–0.8. Adding them lets BM25 drown out meaning search.")
