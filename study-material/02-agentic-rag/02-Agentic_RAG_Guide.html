1

Why RAG exists

A smart model that has never seen your documents is still guessing about them.

A Large Language Model (LLM) like DeepSeek learned from a huge amount of public text. So it knows a lot of general things: what finance is, how Python works, what a Transformer is.

But it has never seen your private documents: your company's internal financial report, your HR policy, your client contracts, your class notes. Those were not in its training data. If you ask about them, it either says "I don't know" or, worse, confidently makes something up.

Think of an open-book exam

The student (the LLM) is intelligent and knows the subject in general. But for questions about one specific textbook, they do much better if they can open the book, find the right page, and answer from it. RAG is how we give the LLM that open book.

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Read the three words backwards and you have the whole idea:

  • Generation: the LLM writes an answer…
  • Augmented: …but we first add extra information to its prompt…
  • Retrieval: …which we fetched (retrieved) from our own documents.

No retraining, no fine-tuning. We simply hand the model the right pages at the moment it needs them.

You have already used RAG. When a bank's chatbot answers "What is the penalty for closing my FD early?" from the bank's own FD rules, or a company's HR bot answers "How many casual leaves do I get?" from the HR policy, that's RAG. The model didn't memorise those rules; it looked them up a second before answering.

Why not just paste the whole document into the prompt? A long PDF can be hundreds of pages. That is slow, costly, and often more than the model can take in at once. RAG sends only the few most useful pieces.

2

Traditional RAG, step by step

Two phases: prepare the library once, then answer questions from it many times.

Step 1: Load

Read the documents. They can be PDFs, Word files, web pages, text files. In this project we use one PDF: the research paper that introduced the Transformer architecture (the "T" in GPT).

Step 2: Split into chunks

Think of notes before an exam

You don't re-read the whole 400-page textbook for one question. You've marked it with sticky notes, one per topic, so you can flip straight to the right page. Chunks are those sticky-noted pages.

We cut the long text into small pieces called chunks, for example 1,000–2,000 characters each. Small pieces let us later pick only the parts that matter. There are different splitting techniques: by fixed size, by paragraph, by heading, and so on. Often consecutive chunks overlap a little so a sentence cut at the edge is not lost.

Try it: chunking
Move the sliders. Each colour is one chunk. Smaller chunks are more precise but carry less context; bigger chunks carry more context but more noise.

Wavy underline = overlap: text repeated from the end of the previous chunk, so nothing is lost at the cut.

Step 3: Embed (turn text into numbers)

Computers can't compare meanings directly, but they are excellent at comparing numbers. An embedding model converts each chunk into a list of numbers called a vector. The model we use, BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, turns every chunk into 384 numbers.

The magic: texts with similar meaning get similar numbers, even if they use different words. "How do I cancel my order?" and "Steps to stop a purchase" land close together.

Think of a city map

Every chunk gets an address (coordinates) on a giant map. Chunks about the same topic live in the same neighbourhood. When a question comes, we find its address and look at the nearest neighbours. Real maps have 2 coordinates; embeddings have hundreds.

You see this idea every day: a music app puts songs that "feel" alike next to each other, so when you like one romantic Hindi song it suggests ten more like it. It isn't matching song titles; it's matching how close they are in its number-space. Embeddings do the same for text.

Try it: semantic search on a meaning map
Each dot is a chunk placed by meaning (squeezed into 2D so we can see it). The gold diamond is your question. Pick one; the 3 nearest chunks are retrieved. Scores here are illustrative.

    Step 4: Store in a vector database

    We save every vector (plus its original text) in a vector database. It is built for one job: given a new vector, quickly find the most similar stored vectors. We use ChromaDB, which runs on your laptop and saves to disk, so indexing happens only once.

    Then, for every question: retrieve and generate

    Top-k is "restaurants near me"

    A maps app doesn't show you every restaurant in the city. It shows the nearest few. Top-k is the same: "show me the k chunks nearest to my question". k = 4 means the 4 closest.

    1. Embed the user's question with the same embedding model.
    2. Ask the vector database for the top-k most similar chunks (k = 4 in our code).
    3. Put the question and those chunks into a prompt and send it to the LLM.
    4. The LLM writes an answer using those chunks.

    Rule you must not break: the question and the documents must be embedded with the same embedding model. Different models create different "maps", and distances between them mean nothing.

    3

    Where plain RAG struggles

    It follows the same fixed path every time, even when that path is wrong.

    • Retrieval can miss. The top-k chunks may be only loosely related. The LLM then answers from the wrong material.
    • It always searches the documents. Ask "What's the weather today?" and it still digs through your PDF, finds nothing useful, and answers badly.
    • It can still hallucinate. RAG reduces made-up answers a lot, but the LLM can still add facts that are not in the chunks.
    • Nobody checks the work. Whatever comes out goes straight to the user.
    A bus with one fixed route

    Plain RAG is a bus that always takes the same road, even when the passenger needs a different destination. Agentic RAG is a metro network with a smart station controller who picks the right line and inspectors who check the train before it reaches the final station.

    4

    Agentic RAG: the big idea

    Replace the fixed pipeline with a small team of AI agents that decide, fetch, and double-check.

    An agent is an LLM given a role, a goal, and permission to use tools. Instead of one model doing everything blindly, we split the work across five specialists. Each is powered by an LLM, and each does one narrow job well.

    AgentIts one jobReal-life twinSays
    1. RouterReads the question and decides where the answer lives: in our PDF, or on the internet.Hospital reception desk that sends you to the right departmentvectorstore or websearch
    2. RetrieverCalls the tool the Router picked and brings back the information.Librarian who fetches the right book, or looks it up onlineThe retrieved answer + evidence
    3. Answer GraderChecks: does this answer actually address the question? Filters out wrong retrievals.Teacher asking "did you answer what was asked?"yes or no
    4. Hallucination GraderChecks: is every claim supported by the source text, or did the model invent something?Auditor asking "show me the receipt for every expense"yes (grounded) or no
    5. Answer GeneratorIf both checks pass, polishes the answer. If either fails, searches the web and answers from that.News anchor who reads out only the checked storyThe final answer

    Two tools

    • RAG tool: the whole traditional RAG pipeline from chapter 2, packed into one function the agents can call.
    • Web search tool: searches the internet and returns the top 3 results.

    Because tools are just functions, you can keep adding more later: a SQL database tool, a graph database tool for customer relationships, a calculator, a company API. That is what makes this design powerful and easy to extend.

    Two LLMs

    The system uses two models with different jobs:

    • Main LLM (DeepSeek, through OpenRouter): the brain of all five agents. It orchestrates the whole workflow.
    • RAG LLM (fast model on Groq): sits inside the RAG tool and turns the retrieved chunks into a short answer. Speed matters here, so we use Groq, which is famous for very fast responses.
    A newsroom

    The editor (main LLM) decides what to cover and approves the final story. A quick junior reporter (RAG LLM) runs to the archive, reads the files, and hands back a short summary. Two fact-checkers (the graders) review it before it goes to print.

    The key shift: in plain RAG, the path is hard-coded. In agentic RAG, an LLM decides the path at run time, and other LLMs verify the result. You trade some speed and cost for better routing and fewer wrong answers.

    Follow one question, end to end

    Like a relay race: each runner does one lap and passes the baton.

    Here is exactly what each agent receives and hands over for the question "What is self-attention?". The texts are shortened, but this is the real shape of what you'll see in your terminal.

    1. Routerthe reception desk
      ReceivesThe question + a one-line note: "our database holds a paper about Transformers, attention, positional encoding…"
      Hands overvectorstore
      Self-attention is a Transformer topic, so the PDF should know.
    2. Retriever + RAG toolthe librarian
      ReceivesThe word vectorstore + the question. Calls rag_tool("What is self-attention?").
      Hands overSOURCE: document. "Self-attention relates different positions of one sentence to build its meaning…" + the 4 chunks it came from.
    3. Answer Graderthe teacher
      ReceivesThe question + the retrieved answer.
      Hands overyes
      The answer talks about self-attention, which is what was asked.
    4. Hallucination Graderthe auditor
      ReceivesThe answer + the 4 source chunks + the relevance grade.
      Hands overyes
      Every sentence in the answer can be traced to a chunk.
    5. Answer Generatorthe news anchor
      ReceivesThe answer + both grades (yes, yes).
      Hands overA clean, short final answer for the user. No mention of routing or grading.

    Same team, different question

    Ask "Who won yesterday's cricket match?" and the baton takes the other track:

    1. Router
      ReceivesThe question + the note about the PDF.
      Hands overwebsearch. A research paper can't know yesterday's score.
    2. Retriever + web tool
      Receiveswebsearch. Calls web_search_tool(...).
      Hands overSOURCE: web. Top 3 results with titles, links and text.
    3. Graders, then Answer Generator
      ReceivesThe web answer + the snippets.
      Hands overBoth checks pass → a short answer built from the search results.

    Real-world use cases

    Same pattern everywhere: private documents for the library line, live data for the web line, checks because mistakes are costly.

    Bank customer support

    Library line
    Loan, FD and credit card policy PDFs, fee schedules.
    Live line
    Today's repo rate news, current offers (web or the bank's own API as a tool).
    Why the checks
    Telling a customer the wrong foreclosure charge leads to complaints and penalties.
    Example question
    "If I close my 2-year FD after 8 months, what penalty do I pay?"

    Hospital help desk

    Library line
    Visiting rules, admission checklist, insurance and cashless process documents.
    Live line
    Doctor availability from the hospital's appointment system (a tool).
    Why the checks
    A made-up instruction about fasting before a test can hurt a patient.
    Example question
    "What documents do I need for cashless admission?"

    HR assistant inside a company

    Library line
    Leave policy, travel policy, reimbursement rules.
    Live line
    This year's public holiday list for your state.
    Why the checks
    Wrong leave rules create payroll and trust problems.
    Example question
    "Can I carry forward unused casual leave to next year?"

    Food delivery or e-commerce support

    Library line
    Refund and cancellation policy, delivery partner guidelines.
    Live line
    Order status from the order system (a tool that calls an internal API).
    Why the checks
    Promising a refund the policy doesn't allow costs real money.
    Example question
    "My order is 40 minutes late, can I cancel for a full refund?"

    College study assistant

    Library line
    Lecture notes, syllabus, previous year papers.
    Live line
    The latest version of a library or tool mentioned in class.
    Why the checks
    A confident wrong answer the night before an exam is the worst kind.
    Example question
    "Explain unit 3's normalisation forms with an example from the notes."

    Legal and contracts team

    Library line
    Signed contracts, internal compliance guidelines.
    Live line
    Recent changes in a law or regulation.
    Why the checks
    Every claim must point to a clause. The hallucination grader acts like a junior lawyer checking citations.
    Example question
    "What is our notice period for ending the vendor contract with this supplier?"

    Plain RAG or agentic RAG? How to choose

    Choose plain RAG when…Choose agentic RAG when…
    All answers live in your documents.Some questions need live data, an API, or the web.
    You need an answer in 1–2 seconds.A few extra seconds are fine in exchange for accuracy.
    Mistakes are cheap (a casual FAQ bot).Mistakes are costly (money, health, legal).
    Budget per question must be near zero.You can afford several LLM calls per question.

    Agent, Task, Tool, Crew

    Four words. Learn them once and every CrewAI project becomes readable.

    Think of a small office

    Agent = an employee (who). Task = an item on their to-do list (what). Tool = equipment they are allowed to use, like a phone or a library card (with what). Crew = the whole team plus the order in which they work (how it runs).

    An agent has three key settings

    • role: the job title. "Router".
    • goal: what success means. "Route user questions to the vectorstore or web search."
    • backstory: who this agent is and how it thinks. "You are an expert at deciding…". You talk to the agent as if it were a person with expertise.

    Plus llm (its brain), allow_delegation (may it hand work to another agent? we say no), and verbose (print its thinking so we can learn from it).

    A task has four key settings

    • description: the instructions, with a {question} placeholder.
    • expected_output: the exact shape of the result ("one word: yes or no").
    • agent: who does it.
    • context: which earlier tasks' outputs it may read. This is how information flows down the line.

    Prompt engineering is the real work

    The backstories and descriptions are prompts. Getting them right takes several rounds of trial and error. Three fixes that came from real testing of this exact project:

    1. The Router kept matching keywords instead of understanding the question. Adding "make routing decisions based on the semantic meaning of the question, not just keyword matching" fixed it.
    2. Graders wrote long explanations, which confused the next agent. Adding "exactly one word: yes or no, no explanation" fixed it.
    3. The final answer explained how it was found instead of just answering. Adding "the final answer contains only the answer, not how you found it" fixed it.

    Lesson: when an agent misbehaves, don't rewrite code first. Read its output in the terminal, then tighten its backstory, description or expected output.

    Plain RAG first

    Before building the team, see what one person working alone can and can't do.

    step1_plain_rag.py is the traditional pipeline from chapter 2 in a few lines: search the PDF, make one LLM call, print the answer. No router, no web, no checks.

    step1_plain_rag.py
    def plain_rag(question: str) -> str:
    # 1. INDEXING (only slow the first time; the vectors are saved on disk)
    library = create_pdf_search_tool(config.PDF_PATH)

    # 2. RETRIEVAL: get the top-k most similar chunks
    chunks = library.run(query=question)
    print("\n----- CHUNKS FOUND (first 600 characters) -----")
    print(chunks[:600], "...")

    # 3. GENERATION: one LLM call with question + chunks
    llm = get_rag_llm()
    answer = clean_text(llm.call(RAG_PROMPT.format(context=chunks, question=question)))
    return answer
    1. It reuses the same library builder as the agentic version, so you're comparing like with like.
    2. It prints the chunks it found, so you can see exactly what the LLM was given.
    3. One LLM call, then done. Fast and cheap.
    terminal
    $ python step1_plain_rag.py "What is multi-head attention?"
$ python step1_plain_rag.py "What is the weather in Delhi today?"

    The first question gets a good answer. The second can't: the PDF knows nothing about Delhi's weather, so the best plain RAG can do is say NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT. There is no other place it can look.

    One shopkeeper vs. a well-run store

    Plain RAG is a single shopkeeper who only checks the shelf behind him. Agentic RAG is a store with a front-desk person who knows whether to check the godown or call the supplier, plus a supervisor who checks the bill before it reaches you.

    Now run the same two questions through main.py (after the next chapters) and compare the answers, the time taken, and the journey line.

    def create_pdf_search_tool(pdf_path: str) -> PDFSearchTool:
    """Build the 'library': load the PDF, chunk it, embed it, store it."""
    # One "shelf" (collection) per PDF + embedding model, so switching to a
    # different PDF or model never mixes old vectors with new ones.
    shelf = f"{Path(pdf_path).stem}_{config.EMBEDDING_MODEL.split('/')[-1]}"
    shelf = re.sub(r"[^a-zA-Z0-9_-]", "_", shelf)[:60]

    return PDFSearchTool(
        pdf=pdf_path,
        collection_name=shelf,
        limit=config.TOP_K,  # bring back the top-k most similar chunks
        config={
            # The embedding model: turns text into numbers (vectors).
            # It runs locally and is downloaded once from Hugging Face.
            "embedding_model": {
                "provider": "sentence-transformer",
                "config": {"model_name": config.EMBEDDING_MODEL},
            },
            # The vector database where the vectors are stored.
            "vectordb": {"provider": "chromadb", "config": {}},
        },
    )
    1. PDFSearchTool from CrewAI does the whole indexing phase for us: load the PDF, split into chunks, embed, store in ChromaDB.
    2. collection_name is the "shelf" in the vector database. We name it after the PDF and the embedding model so that switching either one never mixes old vectors with new ones.
    3. limit=config.TOP_K is our top-k: bring back the 4 most similar chunks (change it in .env).
    4. "sentence-transformer" means the embedding model runs locally on your laptop. It's downloaded once from Hugging Face (about 130 MB).

    The RAG tool: retrieval + fast generation

    tools.py
    def create_rag_tool(pdf_path: str):
    """Wrap the PDF search + the fast RAG LLM into ONE tool the agents can call."""
    pdf_search = create_pdf_search_tool(pdf_path)  # indexing happens here
    rag_llm = get_rag_llm()

    @tool("rag_tool")
    def rag_tool(question: str) -> str:
        """Search the private PDF knowledge base and return an answer grounded
        in the document, followed by the exact text chunks that were used.
        Input must be the user's full question as plain text."""
        # Step A - RETRIEVAL: find the most similar chunks in the vector DB
        chunks = pdf_search.run(query=question)

        if not chunks or "No relevant content found" in chunks:
            return "NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT"

        # Step B - GENERATION: the fast LLM writes an answer from those chunks
        answer = clean_text(rag_llm.call(RAG_PROMPT.format(context=chunks, question=question)))

        if "NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT" in answer:
            return "NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT"
        return f"ANSWER FROM DOCUMENT:\n{answer}\n\nCHUNKS USED:\n{chunks}"

    return rag_tool
    1. We build the library once (pdf_search) and the fast LLM once (rag_llm) outside the inner function, so they are reused on every call.
    2. @tool("rag_tool") turns the inner function into something an agent can call. The agent learns what the tool does by reading its docstring, so the docstring is an instruction, not decoration.
    3. Step A, retrieval: pdf_search.run(query=question) returns the most similar chunks.
    4. Step B, generation: the fast LLM reads only those chunks and writes an answer. The prompt says "use ONLY the context" and "reply NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT if it isn't there".
    5. clean_text removes any <think> rough work. If the fast LLM itself says the answer isn't there, the tool says NOT_FOUND_IN_DOCUMENT so the Retriever knows to try the web.
    6. We return both the answer and the chunks. The chunks are the evidence the Hallucination Grader needs later, like attaching receipts to an expense claim.

    @tool("web_search_tool")
def web_search_tool(question: str) -> str:
    """Search the internet for fresh or general information that is NOT in the
    private PDF. Input must be the user's full question as plain text.
    Returns the top results with their title, link and content."""
    try:
        client = TavilyClient(api_key=config.TAVILY_API_KEY)
        response = client.search(query=question, max_results=config.WEB_RESULTS)
    except Exception as error:  # no internet, wrong key, limit reached ...
        # Return a readable message instead of crashing the whole crew.
        return f"WEB_SEARCH_FAILED: {error}"

    results = response.get("results", [])
    if not results:
        return "NO_WEB_RESULTS"

    lines = []
    for number, item in enumerate(results, start=1):
        lines.append(
            f"[{number}] {item.get('title', '')}\n"
            f"Link: {item.get('url', '')}\n"
            f"{item.get('content', '')}\n"
        )
    return "\n".join(lines)
    1. The plain @tool decorator makes this ordinary Python function an agent tool.
    2. TavilyClient(...).search(query=..., max_results=3) runs the search and returns clean results.
    3. The try / except catches problems like no internet or a wrong key, and returns a readable message instead of crashing the whole team. One broken tool shouldn't stop the whole office.
    4. We format each result as title, link and content so the agents (and you) can see where facts came from.

    A bug worth remembering. In an early version of this project, the web search tool failed with "missing 1 required positional argument". The agent was calling the tool without passing the question. The fix had two parts: the tool's docstring says "Input must be the user's full question", and the task descriptions say "pass it the question: '{question}'". When an agent calls a tool wrongly, fix the instructions it reads.

    def build_crew() -> Crew:
    # ---- 1. TOOLS ---------------------------------------------------------
    rag_tool = create_rag_tool(config.PDF_PATH)  # builds the vector DB (indexing)

    # ---- 2. AGENTS --------------------------------------------------------
    router = create_router_agent()
    retriever = create_retriever_agent()
    grader = create_answer_grader_agent()
    hallucination_grader = create_hallucination_grader_agent()
    answer_generator = create_answer_generator_agent()

    # ---- 3. TASKS (each one can read the output of the ones before it) ----
    router_task = create_router_task(router)
    retriever_task = create_retriever_task(retriever, router_task, rag_tool, web_search_tool)
    grader_task = create_grader_task(grader, retriever_task)
    hallucination_task = create_hallucination_task(hallucination_grader, retriever_task, grader_task)
    answer_task = create_answer_task(

    )

    # max_rpm = at most this many LLM calls per minute (protects free tiers).
    return Crew(
        agents=[router, retriever, grader, hallucination_grader, answer_generator],
        tasks=[router_task, retriever_task, grader_task, hallucination_task, answer_task],
        process=Process.sequential,
        max_rpm=config.MAX_RPM,
        verbose=True,
    )

    2. We create the five agents, then the five tasks, passing each task the earlier tasks it depends on.
    3. Process.sequential: tasks run one after another, in list order. Simple and easy to debug.
    4. max_rpm=config.MAX_RPM: the whole team may make at most 10 LLM calls a minute. If they go faster, CrewAI waits.
    5. verbose=True: print everything each agent thinks and does. Turn it off later for a cleaner output.

    def ask(crew: Crew, question: str) -> str:
    start = time.time()
    # kickoff() starts the team. `inputs` fills every {question} placeholder.
    result = crew.kickoff(inputs={"question": question})
    seconds = time.time() - start
    answer = clean_text(result)

    route, _, relevant, grounded, _ = (one_word(t) for t in crew.tasks)
    summary = f"route={route}  relevant={relevant}  grounded={grounded}  time={seconds:.0f}s"

    print("\n" + "=" * 70)
    print("QUESTION :", question)
    print("ANSWER   :", answer)
    print("JOURNEY  :", summary)
    print("=" * 70)

    # Keep a diary of every question, so you can compare runs later.
    with open(config.ANSWERS_LOG, "a", encoding="utf-8") as log:
        log.write(f"## {datetime.now():%Y-%m-%d %H:%M}  {question}\n\n{answer}\n\n`{summary}`\n\n")
    return answer
    1. crew.kickoff(inputs={"question": question}) starts the team and fills every {question} placeholder.
    2. After the run, every task's result is still available. We read the one-word results of the Router and both graders to print a journey line, like a train ticket showing the stations you passed.
    3. Every question is appended to answers_log.md: your diary of experiments.

    def download_pdf(url: str, save_path: str) -> None:
    """Download the PDF only if we don't already have it."""
    if os.path.exists(save_path):
        print(f"PDF already present: {save_path}")
        return
    os.makedirs(os.path.dirname(save_path) or ".", exist_ok=True)
    print(f"Downloading PDF from {url} ...")
    request = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={"User-Agent": "Mozilla/5.0"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(request, timeout=60) as response, open(save_path, "wb") as f:
        f.write(response.read())
    print(f"Saved to {save_path}")

    Why is it slow?

    One question means roughly 7–8 LLM calls (5 agents, a tool call, and the RAG LLM). On fast providers the whole flow takes roughly 15–30 seconds. On free or overloaded models it can take several minutes; on a slow free model it once took about 10 minutes. On a laptop with Ollama, expect minutes too. Speed depends on the provider, its rate limits, and your hardware.

    Free tiers have small limits. Free plans allow only a limited number of requests per minute and per day. Since each question uses 7–8 calls, you can hit the limit after a handful of questions. If you see a 429 error, wait a minute, lower MAX_RPM, or use a paid model.

    Retries are normal

    Sometimes an agent hits an error (a tool call in the wrong format, a temporary API failure) and you'll see it try again. CrewAI retries automatically. Only worry if the same error repeats until the run stops.

    Questions learners ask

    Answers to the questions that come up every time this is taught.

    How does the Router know whether the answer is in the document, if it never reads it?

    It judges the meaning of the question against what it has been told the vector database contains (the PDF_TOPIC line in its backstory). It's an educated guess, not a lookup. The safety nets are the Retriever's fallback (if the RAG tool returns nothing, go to the web) and the two graders. A stronger design gives the Router a quick "peek" tool to check the vector database before deciding.

    What exactly does the RAG tool do?

    Everything from chapter 2 in one function. When created, it does the indexing (load, chunk, embed, store). When called, it does retrieval (find similar chunks) and a quick generation (the fast LLM writes an answer from them). The agents around it decide when to use it and check what it returns.

    What is a vector database, and why not use SQL?

    Text is first split into tokens, and models represent meaning as long lists of numbers. Large models use thousands of numbers per token; our small embedding model uses 384 per chunk. A normal SQL database is great at exact matches ("find customer 42") but bad at "find the 4 rows whose 384 numbers are closest to these 384 numbers". A vector database is built and indexed for exactly that similarity search.

    How are Agents, Tasks and Tools related?

    The task says what to do, the agent does it, and the tool helps the agent reach outside itself (a PDF, the web, an API). You give an agent a task and a tool to achieve that task's goal. They complement each other.

    Can an agent pull data from a website or a company API?

    Yes. Write a Python function that calls the API or scrapes the page (for example with requests and BeautifulSoup, or a browser tool like Selenium), decorate it with @tool, give it a clear docstring, and add it to a task. There's practically no limit to the tools you can add.

    Where does this code run: front end or back end?

    Back end. It's Python. Here we run it from the command line; in a real product you'd wrap crew.kickoff() in an API (for example with FastAPI) and a front end would call that API.

    How would I deploy this for real users?

    • Simple start: put the API on a cloud server such as an AWS EC2 machine. Add a GPU only if you host the LLM yourself.
    • Many users at once: run several copies behind a load balancer, and use a queue for long-running jobs, since each answer takes seconds to minutes.
    • Models: APIs are fastest to ship; self-hosting helps with privacy and cost at scale but needs more maintenance. It depends on your timeline and requirements.

    Can I use this for my company's documents?

    Yes. Put your PDF in data/, set PDF_PATH and a good PDF_TOPIC. For many documents, point the tool at a folder or build one library per department. Before real use, test with 20–30 real questions from your users and check the answers by hand. If the documents are confidential, run the brain locally with Ollama or use a provider your company has approved.

    Why two different LLMs?

    Different jobs need different strengths. The orchestrator needs good reasoning. The RAG step is simple ("summarise these chunks") but happens in the middle of the flow, so a very fast model keeps the whole run snappy.

    Glossary

    LLMLarge Language Model. Predicts and writes text. DeepSeek, Llama and GPT are examples.
    RAGRetrieval-Augmented Generation. Fetch relevant text from your documents and give it to the LLM with the question.
    Agentic RAGRAG where AI agents decide the path (which source to use) and verify the result.
    AgentAn LLM with a role, a goal, a backstory and permission to use tools.
    TaskA unit of work for an agent: description, expected output, context.
    ToolA function an agent can call, like searching a PDF or the web.
    CrewThe team of agents plus their tasks and the order they run in.
    ChunkA small piece of a document.
    Embedding / vectorA list of numbers that represents the meaning of a text.
    Vector databaseA database optimised to store vectors and find the most similar ones fast.
    Semantic searchSearching by meaning instead of exact words.
    Top-kThe number of most-similar chunks we retrieve (k = 4 here).
    HallucinationWhen an LLM states something that is not true or not supported by its sources.
    GroundedSupported by the source text.
    InferenceRunning a trained model to get an answer.
    Tool calling (function calling)An LLM's ability to say "please run this function with these inputs" in a structured format.
    TokenA small piece of text (often part of a word) that LLMs read and write. Pricing and limits are counted in tokens.
    Rate limitThe maximum number of requests a provider allows per minute or per day.
    API keyA secret password that lets your code use a paid or free online service.
    Virtual environmentA private folder of Python libraries for one project.
