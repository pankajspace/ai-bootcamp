The whole talk in one picture

An agent can sound confident and still be wrong, and asking the same thing twice can give two different answers. So "it looked fine when I tried it" isn't evidence. Evaluation means: write down what "good" means for your agent, build a set of realistic test questions, score every part of the agent (prompt, retrieval, tools, routing), read the failures to find patterns, and make sure the people (or the LLM judge) doing the scoring actually agree with each other.

Part 1

Why evaluating an agent is hard

Slide 1

Evaluating your Agent: is your agent doing its job?

by Shivank Agarwal

💡 In simple words

Building an agent is the easy half. The hard half is proving it works: on real questions, every time, for every part of the system. This talk is about that second half: evaluation, or "evals" for short.

🍛 Analogy: a new employee's probation

You don't confirm a new hire because their first answer sounded smart. You watch them on many real tasks, check the work against what a good result looks like, ask a couple of senior people for their opinion, and notice the mistakes they keep repeating. Evaluating an agent is the same, written down and repeatable.

Slide 2

Why agents make mistakes: the three gulfs

▢ What the slide says

Three gaps sit between the developer, the data and the LLM:

  • Gulf of Comprehension (developer ↔ data): developers struggle to fully understand their input data and how the pipeline behaves across different scenarios.
  • Gulf of Specification (developer → LLM): what the user wants is often loosely written in prompts, leading to unclear tasks and inconsistent results.
  • Gulf of Generalization (data → LLM): models behave unpredictably across different inputs, with biases and errors appearing in production.

🍛 Analogy: a new cook at your dhaba

  • Comprehension: you've never looked at what customers actually order, so you train the cook on the wrong dishes.
  • Specification: you tell the cook "make it tasty". Tasty for whom? How spicy? Vague instructions, inconsistent food.
  • Generalization: the cook nails dal makhani in training, then a customer orders it with no onion and no garlic, and it all falls apart.

🌍 In the code

prompt_evals_inventory.py is a gulf-of-specification experiment. Version 1 of the prompt says only "You are an inventory management advisor. Answer the question." Version 2 spells out what a good answer contains (the principle, the real-world complication, the hidden misconception). The eval measures how much that clearer specification helps: in the example run, from 63% to 96% of checks passed.

Slide 3

Why traditional testing falls short for agents

▢ What the slide says

Non-deterministic
Unlike normal software, LLMs can produce different outputs for the same input.
Subjective quality
Whether a response is good depends on context, what the user wanted, and domain expertise.
Multifaceted
Success means checking accuracy, relevance, safety and user satisfaction at the same time.

These make agent evaluation fundamentally different from classic software testing and ML testing.

💡 In simple words

A normal unit test has one right answer and checks it exactly:

assert add(2, 2) == 4          # same input, same output, every time

You can't write assert agent("Should we reorder?") == "...", because there are many good answers, worded differently each time, and "good" depends on who's asking. Classic ML testing doesn't fit either: it measures accuracy against one label per example, not "is this paragraph helpful, correct and safe?"

⌨️ Try it in code: same question, three times

s03_same_question.pyNeeds OpenAI key
"""Slide 3: ask the same question three times and compare the answers."""
from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()
client = OpenAI()
question = "Suggest one name for a stock-tracking app for small kirana stores. Reply with the name only."

answers = []
for attempt in range(1, 4):
    reply = client.chat.completions.create(model="gpt-6-luna", reasoning_effort="none",
                                           messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}])
    answers.append(reply.choices[0].message.content.strip())
    print(f"Attempt {attempt}: {answers[-1]}")
print(f"\nSame input, {len(set(answers))} different answers.")
Example output (what you would see; exact text and numbers vary)
Attempt 1: StockSaathi
Attempt 2: KiranaKhata
Attempt 3: DukaanDost

Same input, 3 different answers.

Three valid answers, all different. That's fine for brainstorming, but it means one run proves nothing: you need many test cases, and checks that accept any correct answer rather than one exact string.

🍛 Analogy: maths exam vs essay exam

A maths paper has an answer key: 42 is right, 41 is wrong. An essay paper needs a marking scheme ("mentions three causes", "gives an example"), and two teachers may still give different marks. Agents are essay exams.

Slide 4

What agentic evaluation needs

▢ What the slide says

  • No universal solutions: each pipeline has to bridge the three gulfs in its own way.
  • Context-specific metrics: success depends on your goals, not generic benchmarks.
  • Evolving requirements: what you measure changes as the system and its users mature.
  • Resource intensive: meaningful evaluation takes real time and domain expertise.
  • Multiple components: each component of an agent needs its own evaluation.

Generic benchmarks often miss the real-world problems that matter most to your users.

🍛 Analogy: a car's annual service

The mechanic doesn't just drive round the block and say "seems fine". Brakes, tyres, battery and lights are each checked separately, with their own test. A car can have perfect brakes and a dead battery. An agent can have a great prompt and a broken router.

⌨️ Try it in code: evaluate just the router

The router in langgraph_router.py is one component. This test calls only the routing step on 9 questions with known correct routes, so a wrong answer can't hide a wrong route (or the other way round).

s04_router_eval.pyNeeds OpenAI key
"""Slides 4 and 6: evaluate ONE component on its own. Did the router send each question to the right specialist?
Put this file next to langgraph_router.py."""
from collections import Counter

from langgraph_router import router                 # only the routing step, no specialist answers

labelled = [
    ("Stock is 120 and we sell 20 a day with a 4-day lead time. Do we order today?", "reorder"),
    ("How much safety stock should I keep for 95% service?", "reorder"),
    ("What order size minimises our total cost?", "reorder"),
    ("Diwali is coming. How should next month's demand estimate change?", "forecast"),
    ("Sales have grown 5% every month this year. What should I plan for Q1?", "forecast"),
    ("Is a 3-week moving average better than a 6-week one for fast movers?", "forecast"),
    ("Vendor X is 8% cheaper but late one delivery in four. Should we switch?", "supplier"),
    ("Which of our two packaging suppliers has the lower total cost?", "supplier"),
    ("Our supplier keeps delivering late. Should I raise my reorder point?", "reorder"),
]

correct, confusion = 0, Counter()
for question, expected in labelled:
    got = router({"query": question})["category"]
    correct += got == expected
    confusion[(expected, got)] += 1
    print(f"{'✅' if got == expected else '❌'} {got:<9} (expected {expected:<9}) {question}")

print(f"\nRouting accuracy: {correct}/{len(labelled)} = {correct / len(labelled):.0%}")
labels = ["reorder", "forecast", "supplier"]
print("\nConfusion (rows = expected, columns = routed to)")
print(" " * 10 + "".join(f"{l:>10}" for l in labels))
for e in labels:
    print(f"{e:<10}" + "".join(f"{confusion[(e, g)]:>10}" for g in labels))

🌍 Real world: why generic benchmarks miss things

A model can score highly on a public reasoning benchmark and still not know that stock arriving tomorrow counts when deciding to reorder. Only your questions, with your idea of a good answer, can catch that.

Part 2

Metrics: what to measure

Slide 5

Evaluation metrics overview: two main categories

▢ What the slide says

Core purpose: measure the quality and reliability of an LLM pipeline in a systematic way, across different use cases.

  • Reference-based metrics compare the LLM's output with a known correct answer, called the "reference" or "golden" output.
  • Reference-free metrics judge the output by its own properties, or whether it follows rules, with no golden answer.

Which one you pick depends on what you're evaluating and what resources you have. Both have their own uses and trade-offs. (The appendix, slides 18–21, goes deeper into each.)

🍛 Analogy: two ways to check homework

Reference-based: the teacher has the answer key and compares. Reference-free: no answer key for an essay, so the teacher checks rules instead: is it on topic, under 500 words, no copied lines?

💡 The metrics that need a definition

  • Precision@k: of the k passages you retrieved, how many were useful? Recall@k: of the passages you needed, how many did you retrieve? Example from the RAG eval: a question needs passages [0, 1]; you retrieve [1, 11, 7]. Precision@3 = 1/3 (only passage 1 was useful), Recall@3 = 1/2 (you missed passage 0).
  • Groundedness / hallucination rate: does every claim in the answer come from the retrieved text? A hallucination rate of 4% means 4 answers in 100 said something the sources don't support.
  • Cache hit rate: how often a repeated question was answered from a saved result instead of calling the model again (faster and cheaper).
  • Tool match accuracy: how often the agent picked the tool you expected.

⌨️ Try it in code: the System row, latency and cost

s06_latency_cost.pyNeeds OpenAI key
"""Slide 6, the System row: measure latency and cost per 1,000 queries."""
import time

from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()
client = OpenAI()
INPUT_PRICE_PER_1M, OUTPUT_PRICE_PER_1M = 0.10, 0.50       # gpt-6-luna, USD (openai.com/api/pricing)

queries = [
    "What is a reorder point, in one sentence?",
    "We sell 40 units a day and lead time is 6 days. What is the reorder point without safety stock?",
    "Name two reasons to keep safety stock.",
    "What does EOQ balance?",
    "When is FEFO used?",
]
latencies, tokens_in, tokens_out = [], 0, 0
for q in queries:
    start = time.perf_counter()
    reply = client.chat.completions.create(model="gpt-6-luna", reasoning_effort="none",
                                           messages=[{"role": "user", "content": q}])
    latencies.append(time.perf_counter() - start)
    tokens_in += reply.usage.prompt_tokens
    tokens_out += reply.usage.completion_tokens

n = len(queries)
cost = tokens_in / 1e6 * INPUT_PRICE_PER_1M + tokens_out / 1e6 * OUTPUT_PRICE_PER_1M
print(f"Queries             : {n}")
print(f"Avg response time   : {1000 * sum(latencies) / n:.0f} ms (slowest {1000 * max(latencies):.0f} ms)")
print(f"Tokens per query    : {tokens_in / n:.0f} in, {tokens_out / n:.0f} out")
print(f"Cost per 1000 queries = ${cost / n * 1000:.3f}")
Example output (what you would see; exact text and numbers vary)
Queries             : 5
Avg response time   : 840 ms (slowest 1320 ms)
Tokens per query    : 22 in, 48 out
Cost per 1000 queries = $0.026

The slide's "$1.20 per 1,000 queries" is just an example. With gpt-6-luna at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, short answers cost a few cents per thousand. Longer prompts (RAG context, tool definitions) push it up, which is why cost is measured, not guessed.

🍛 Analogy: a restaurant's dashboard

Kitchen speed (latency), cost per plate (cost), whether the chef follows the recipe (prompting), whether the right ingredients come out of the store room (RAG), whether the order is completed (planning), and whether the right utensils are used (tools). One number can't describe a restaurant.

Part 3

Test data and error analysis

Slide 7

Tips for generating synthetic test queries

▢ What the slide says

  • Common pitfall: asking an LLM for test queries with no structure gives repetitive, generic questions.
  • Step 1, define dimensions: the ways user behaviour varies. Product search: category, price range, brand. Customer support: issue type, mood, order context.
  • Step 2, identify failures: aim at likely failure modes, found by testing or observation.
  • Step 3, manual tuples: write about 20 combinations by hand, one value per dimension, e.g. (Electronics, Premium, Specific brand).
  • Step 4, scale with an LLM: generate more structured tuples, then turn each tuple into a natural question, e.g. "Show me the latest high-end Bose headphones under ₹30,000."
  • Tip: keep tuple generation and question writing as two separate steps, to avoid repetition.

🍛 Analogy: the exam paper blueprint

A good paper-setter doesn't write 50 random questions. They use a blueprint: chapters × difficulty × question type, and fill every cell. Ask an LLM "give me 50 questions" and you get 50 versions of the easiest chapter.

⌨️ Try it in code: dimensions → tuples → questions

The tuples are made by plain Python (every combination, then a spread of them). Only the last step uses the model, to phrase each tuple like a real customer would.

s07_synthetic_queries.pyNeeds OpenAI key
"""Slide 7: dimensions -> tuples -> natural queries, for a shopping app's support agent."""
import itertools
import json
import random

from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()
client = OpenAI()

# Step 1: dimensions = the ways real users differ
dimensions = {
    "issue": ["late delivery", "wrong item", "refund status", "damaged product", "payment failed"],
    "mood": ["calm", "frustrated", "angry", "confused"],
    "order_context": ["first order", "repeat customer", "order above ₹5,000", "cash on delivery"],
}

# Steps 2–3: every combination is a tuple; pick a spread of them (fixed seed = same picks every run)
all_tuples = list(itertools.product(*dimensions.values()))
random.seed(7)
tuples = random.sample(all_tuples, 8)
print(f"{len(all_tuples)} possible tuples, using {len(tuples)}\n")

# Step 4: ONLY NOW ask the model to phrase each tuple as a real customer message
prompt = ("Write one realistic customer-support chat message for each tuple (issue, mood, order context). "
          "Vary the wording and length like real customers in India do; don't mention the mood word itself. "
          'Reply as JSON: {"queries": ["...", "..."]} in the same order.\n\n'
          + "\n".join(f"{i + 1}. {t}" for i, t in enumerate(tuples)))
reply = client.chat.completions.create(model="gpt-6-luna", reasoning_effort="none",
                                       response_format={"type": "json_object"},
                                       messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
queries = json.loads(reply.choices[0].message.content)["queries"]
for t, q in zip(tuples, queries):
    print(f"{str(t)}\n   → {q}\n")
Example output (what you would see; exact text and numbers vary)
80 possible tuples, using 8

('refund status', 'angry', 'repeat customer')
   → I've ordered from you guys for 2 years and this is the first time my refund is stuck for 12 days. Where is my money??

('wrong item', 'calm', 'cash on delivery')
   → Hi, I received a different shampoo than the one I ordered (COD). How can I exchange it?

('damaged product', 'calm', 'order above ₹5,000')
   → The air fryer I ordered arrived with a cracked lid. It was a ₹6,499 order. Can you arrange a replacement please?

('late delivery', 'frustrated', 'order above ₹5,000')

Every message is clearly different, and you can see exactly which situation each one tests. If the agent fails on all the "angry + repeat customer" messages, you've found a failure mode, not a one-off.

🌍 Real world

Before launch, a support bot rarely has real user logs. Teams generate a few hundred structured test messages this way, then replace them with real (anonymised) user messages as soon as those exist. The dimensions stay useful: they tell you which kinds of user you haven't tested yet.

Slide 8

Error analysis

▢ What the slide says

Error analysis is the systematic process of examining model failures to identify recurring patterns, and using them to guide targeted improvements. It's a loop:

Define evaluation criteria
→
Build baseline agent
→
Collect expert feedback
→
Extract failure modes
→
Analyse failure frequency
→
Improve prompt based on evidence
↺

💡 In simple words

Don't fix the last mistake you happened to see. Read many failures, give each one a short label, count the labels, and fix the most common one first. Then run the evals again to check the fix worked and didn't break anything else.

🍛 Analogy: a hospital's case review

Doctors don't react to one bad outcome by changing every procedure. They review cases together, note what went wrong in each, and look for patterns ("five of eight readmissions missed the same follow-up test"). Then they change the one step that matters most.

⌨️ Try it in code: from reviewers' notes to the top failure mode

These are the notes a reviewer wrote on failed answers from the prompt eval (slide 9's annotator collects exactly this). Each note gets a short code, codes are grouped into categories, and the categories are counted.

s08_failure_modes.pyRan it ✓ real output below
"""Slides 8 and 23: from reviewers' notes to the most common failure modes (error analysis)."""
from collections import Counter

# Step 1 (open coding): a reviewer read each failed answer and wrote a note plus a short code.
failed = [
    ("Q1 PO arriving tomorrow",     "Ignores the PO arriving tomorrow.",                       "ignores in-transit stock"),
    ("Q2 Demand jumped 40%",        "'Keep an eye on demand' is not an action.",               "vague, no action"),
    ("Q4 ROP of 70 units",          "Confirms 70 without safety stock.",                       "accepts the user's wrong formula"),
    ("Q5 Perishable, 8-day cycle",  "Says 8 < 10 days is fine; no FEFO.",                      "accepts the user's wrong assumption"),
    ("Q6 Class C item",             "Treats every C item as unimportant.",                     "accepts the user's wrong assumption"),
    ("Q7 Supplier A vs B",          "Never says which supplier to pick.",                      "vague, no action"),
    ("Q8 Never stock out",          "Says 'pick a service level' but not how.",                "vague, no action"),
    ("Q2 (second run)",             "Raises ROP by 40% straight away.",                        "accepts the user's wrong assumption"),
    ("Q5 (second run)",             "Long answer; action buried in the last line.",            "too long to act on"),
]

# Step 2 (axial coding): group the codes into a few bigger categories.
category_of = {
    "ignores in-transit stock":             "Misses the hidden edge case",
    "accepts the user's wrong formula":     "Misses the hidden edge case",
    "accepts the user's wrong assumption":  "Misses the hidden edge case",
    "vague, no action":                     "Not actionable",
    "too long to act on":                   "Not actionable",
}

# Step 3: count, so you fix the most frequent problem first.
by_code = Counter(code for _, _, code in failed)
by_category = Counter(category_of[code] for _, _, code in failed)

print("Failure codes (open coding):")
for code, n in by_code.most_common():
    print(f"  {n}  {code}")
print("\nFailure categories (axial coding):")
for cat, n in by_category.most_common():
    print(f"  {n}/{len(failed)}  {'█' * n}  {cat}")
top = by_category.most_common(1)[0][0]
print(f"\nCore problem to fix first (selective coding): {top}")
print("→ Prompt change to test next: 'Before answering, name the assumption hidden in the question and say whether it holds.'")
Output (real run)
Failure codes (open coding):
  3  vague, no action
  3  accepts the user's wrong assumption
  1  ignores in-transit stock
  1  accepts the user's wrong formula
  1  too long to act on

Failure categories (axial coding):

  4/9  ████  Not actionable

Core problem to fix first (selective coding): Misses the hidden edge case

The evidence says the biggest problem is missing the hidden edge case, not tone or length. So the next prompt change targets that, and the prompt eval is run again to check the pass rate goes up. This labelling method is called grounded theory, explained on slide 23.

Design a custom data annotator to speed up error analysis

▢ What the slide says

💡 Why build your own instead of using a spreadsheet?

Reading 200 long answers in spreadsheet cells is slow and tiring, and tired reviewers get sloppy. A screen that shows one answer at a time, with big buttons and keyboard shortcuts, makes review several times faster. And because it's your screen, it shows exactly what your reviewers need (here: the reference answer, hidden until they want it).

- Keyboard: P = pass, F = fail, arrow keys to move. Handle commas and line breaks in CSV fields.

⚠️ Careful: hide the judge's score while reviewing

The CSV also contains the LLM judge's scores. The annotator deliberately doesn't show them: if reviewers see "Judge: PASS" first, they tend to agree with it, and you lose an independent second opinion. Compare the two afterwards (slide 15).

Part 4

Human reviewers: dealing with opinions

Slide 10

How to deal with the subjectivity of reviewers

▢ What the slide says

Two people taste the same piece of cheese: one says "Yummy!", the other "Yucky!". Neither is wrong. Solution: ask the neighbours, i.e. use collaborative evaluation, or appoint a benevolent dictator.

💡 The two options

Collaborative evaluation
Several reviewers label the same examples, measure how much they agree, discuss the disagreements and refine the rules until they agree. Slides 11–12 give the steps. Best when the decision really is shared, or reviewers come from different teams.
Benevolent dictator
One trusted domain expert makes the final call, using a written rubric. Faster and more consistent. Best for small teams, or when one person clearly knows the domain best (the head chef decides what "tasty" means on this menu).

🍛 Analogy: judging a cooking competition

Three judges taste each dish and score it on a sheet with fixed criteria (taste, presentation, creativity). If one judge always gives 9s and another always gives 5s, the organisers notice and talk to them. That's collaborative evaluation with a rubric.

🌍 Real world

For the inventory advisor, a warehouse manager and a demand planner may disagree about whether an answer is "clear enough". Writing down "clear = a manager could act on it without asking anything else" turns a matter of taste into a rule both can apply.

Slide 11

Collaborative evaluation process (1 of 2)

▢ What the slide says

A systematic way to build agreement and a high-quality labelled dataset:

  1. Assemble the team: a few domain experts and key stakeholders, for different perspectives.
  2. Draft an initial rubric: clear criteria with specific pass/fail examples that show the edge cases and where the line is.
  3. Select a shared dataset: 20–50 varied, challenging examples covering what reviewers will see in production.
  4. Independent annotation: each person labels the dataset alone, without discussion, so their own interpretations and the unclear spots show up.

💡 What a first rubric looks like (inventory advisor)

CriterionPASS exampleFAIL example
Correct"Don't reorder: the PO arriving tomorrow keeps you above 200.""Reorder now to be safe."
Actionable"Recalculate using the last 6 weeks' average.""Keep an eye on demand."
Handles the hidden edge caseMentions in-transit stock, shelf life, criticality…Answers only the literal question.

🍛 Analogy: board-exam moderation

Before marking thousands of papers, examiners all mark the same sample papers alone, then meet to compare. Where they differ, the marking scheme gets clearer ("give the mark if the unit is missing but the number is right").

Slide 12

Collaborative evaluation process (2 of 2)

▢ What the slide says

  1. Measure agreement: compute Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) to see how much reviewers agree and where the rubric needs work.
  2. Alignment session: discuss the disagreements, understand each person's reasoning, and refine the rubric together.
  3. Revise and iterate: update the rubric, re-label with the new guidelines, and measure IAA again.
  4. Finalise the rubric and gold set: a documented rubric plus a consensus-labelled golden dataset, ready for testing and quality checks.

This loop builds consistency through structured collaboration and produces the labelled data reliable evaluation is built on.

Slide 13

Why measure the agreement expected by chance?

▢ What the slide says

To know whether an evaluation is reliable, look beyond the surface agreement rate. When two evaluators agree, you need to know whether that's real consensus or just statistical coincidence.

💡 In simple words

Two reviewers who both say "pass" to almost everything will agree most of the time, even if they never really read the answers. So "we agree 80% of the time" might mean very little. You have to subtract the agreement they'd get by luck.

🍛 Analogy: the lazy true/false students

A test has 10 true/false questions and 8 answers are "True". Two students who don't study and just write "True" for everything both score 8/10, and agree on all 10 answers. 100% agreement, zero knowledge. Chance agreement is exactly this effect.

Slide 14

The problem with raw agreement

▢ What the slide says

Why raw numbers mislead: two evaluators often agree without truly being aligned. Shared habits, like marking most responses "acceptable", inflate the agreement score. A raw agreement percentage isn't a reliable signal of annotation quality.

Expected agreement by chance: how often would the evaluators agree if each labelled on their own, based only on how often they each use each label? This is the baseline you get from probability alone, not from real consensus.

🧪 Move the sliders: how much of the agreement is real?

Try setting both reviewers to 95% "Acceptable" with 90% raw agreement: kappa drops below zero. They agree less than two people labelling at random with those habits would.

Slide 15

A real-world example: Cohen's kappa

▢ What the slide says

  1. Annotator tendencies: Annotator 1 says Acceptable 60% / Unacceptable 40%. Annotator 2 says Acceptable 65% / Unacceptable 35%.
  2. Calculate chance: both Acceptable = 0.60 × 0.65 = 0.39; both Unacceptable = 0.40 × 0.35 = 0.14; total chance agreement = 53%.
  3. True alignment revealed: if raw agreement is 80%, only 27% reflects real consensus after removing the 53% chance baseline.

Key takeaway: Cohen's kappa removes chance agreement to show how reliable your human or LLM-judge evaluations really are.

⚠️ Careful: the 27% is not kappa

The slide's 27% is percentage points above chance (80 − 53). Cohen's kappa divides that by the most agreement that was possible above chance: (0.80 − 0.53) / (1 − 0.53) = 0.57. Both numbers are useful, but when someone says "kappa", they mean 0.57. A common reading of kappa: below 0.20 slight, 0.21–0.40 fair, 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, above 0.80 almost perfect. So these two annotators are in moderate agreement.

Can we trust the LLM judge? Compare its verdicts with a human reviewer using Cohen's kappa.
By Shivank Agarwal. No model calls: this only reads a CSV.

How to use:
1. Run prompt_evals_inventory.py      -> prompt_eval_results.csv (the judge's scores)
2. Open annotator.html, upload that CSV, mark each answer 👍 / 👎, click "Download feedback"
                                       -> prompt_eval_results_feedback.csv
3. python judge_agreement.py prompt_eval_results_feedback.csv

The judge "passes" an answer only if every score column is 1. The human label is pass / fail.
"""
import csv
import sys

SCORE_COLUMNS = ["accuracy", "clarity", "completeness",                                  # prompt evals
                 "context_relevance", "answer_groundedness", "answer_correctness"]       # RAG evals


def cohens_kappa(a, b):
    """Agreement between two raters, after removing the agreement expected by chance."""
    n = len(a)
    observed = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    labels = set(a) | set(b)
    chance = sum((a.count(l) / n) * (b.count(l) / n) for l in labels)
    kappa = (observed - chance) / (1 - chance) if chance < 1 else 1.0
    return observed, chance, kappa


def describe(k):
    """Common reading of kappa values (Landis & Koch)."""
    for limit, word in [(0, "poor"), (0.20, "slight"), (0.40, "fair"), (0.60, "moderate"), (0.80, "substantial")]:
        if k <= limit:
            return word
    return "almost perfect"


if __name__ == "__main__":
    path = sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "prompt_eval_results_feedback.csv"
    with open(path, encoding="utf-8") as f:
        rows = [r for r in csv.DictReader(f) if r.get("human_label") in ("pass", "fail")]
    if not rows:
        sys.exit("No rows with a human_label of pass/fail. Annotate the file in annotator.html first.")

    scores = [c for c in SCORE_COLUMNS if c in rows[0]]
    judge = ["pass" if all(r[c] == "1" for c in scores) else "fail" for r in rows]
    human = [r["human_label"] for r in rows]

    print(f"File: {path}  ({len(rows)} reviewed answers)")
    print(f"Judge passes an answer when all of these are 1: {', '.join(scores)}\n")

    print("                 Human: pass   Human: fail")
    for j in ("pass", "fail"):
        cells = [sum(1 for x, y in zip(judge, human) if x == j and y == h) for h in ("pass", "fail")]
        print(f"  Judge: {j:<5}   {cells[0]:>10}   {cells[1]:>11}")

    observed, chance, kappa = cohens_kappa(judge, human)
    print(f"\nRaw agreement        : {observed:.0%}")
    print(f"Agreement by chance  : {chance:.0%}   (judge passes {judge.count('pass')}/{len(rows)}, "
          f"human passes {human.count('pass')}/{len(rows)})")
    print(f"Cohen's kappa        : {kappa:.2f}  → {describe(kappa)} agreement")

    disagreements = [(r, j, h) for r, j, h in zip(rows, judge, human) if j != h]
    if disagreements:
        print(f"\nDisagreements to discuss ({len(disagreements)}):")

Slide 18

Reference-based metrics: definition and approach

▢ What the slide says

  • Compare the LLM's output with a known correct answer, the "reference" or "golden" output.
  • You must prepare correct reference answers for your test data in advance, like an official answer key for grading tests.
  • Valuable during development as unit tests, and usually used in offline testing (before release).

💡 In simple words

Write the answer key once, then every time you change the prompt, the model or the retrieval, rerun the whole test set and compare. If yesterday 9/9 passed and today 7/9 pass, your change broke something, and you know which two questions to look at.

🌍 In the code

All three eval files are reference-based at heart: dataset in the prompt eval (each question with its "a" key points), eval_dataset in the RAG eval (each with a reference and its relevant_docs), and test_scenarios in the tool eval (each with its expected_tools).

Slide 19

Reference-based metrics: examples

▢ What the slide says

Simple
Exact string matching: does the output exactly match the reference? Good for short answers where precision is critical.
Keyword verification: does the output contain the key words from the reference? More flexible, allows different wording.
Both suit objective comparisons with few acceptable variations.
Complex
Execution-based: run the generated SQL and compare its result table with the known correct result; run generated code against unit tests.
Hybrid: run the generated code first, then let an oracle (LLM judge or human) judge its behaviour against the intended reference behaviour.

⌨️ Try it in code: exact match, keywords, and running SQL

"""Slides 18–19: grading against a known correct answer (the 'answer key')."""
import sqlite3

print("1) EXACT MATCH: short answers where only one form is right")
cases = [("Which ABC class is 5% of annual spend?", "C", "C"),
         ("Which ABC class is 75% of annual spend?", "A", "Class A")]
for question, reference, output in cases:
    ok = output.strip().lower() == reference.lower()
    print(f"  {'PASS' if ok else 'FAIL'}  expected {reference!r}, got {output!r}")

print("\n2) KEYWORD CHECK: the answer must mention the key facts, in any wording")
reference_keywords = ["in-transit", "overstock"]
answers = {
    "Answer A": "No. With the PO arriving tomorrow, in-transit stock pushes you above the ROP; ordering now risks overstock.",
    "Answer B": "No, 210 is above 200, so wait.",
}
for name, text in answers.items():
    found = [k for k in reference_keywords if k in text.lower()]
    print(f"  {'PASS' if len(found) == len(reference_keywords) else 'FAIL'}  {name}: found {found} of {reference_keywords}")

print("\n3) EXECUTION-BASED: run the generated SQL and compare the RESULT with the reference result")
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE stock (sku TEXT, on_hand INT, reorder_point INT);
INSERT INTO stock VALUES ('SKU-001', 120, 150), ('SKU-002', 400, 200), ('SKU-003', 90, 90), ('SKU-004', 30, 60);
""")
reference_sql = "SELECT sku FROM stock WHERE on_hand <= reorder_point ORDER BY sku"
generated = {
    "Model SQL 1": "SELECT sku FROM stock WHERE reorder_point >= on_hand ORDER BY sku",   # different text, same result
    "Model SQL 2": "SELECT sku FROM stock WHERE on_hand < reorder_point ORDER BY sku",    # misses the 'equal' case
}
expected = db.execute(reference_sql).fetchall()
print(f"  reference result: {[r[0] for r in expected]}")
for name, sql in generated.items():
    got = db.execute(sql).fetchall()
    print(f"  {'PASS' if got == expected else 'FAIL'}  {name}: {[r[0] for r in got]}")

Slide 20

Reference-free metrics

▢ What the slide says

Judge the output by its own properties or by rules, without golden answers. Crucial for subjective, creative or open-ended outputs with many valid answers. Goal: check the desired properties of the output directly.

Text summarisation
The summary adds no speculative claims and no opinions that aren't in the source.
Customer-service chatbot
The bot avoids definitive medical or financial advice and points the user to an expert.
Code generation
Non-trivial logic has explanatory comments; no deprecated functions.
Marketing copy
A clear call to action; no overly aggressive sales phrases that clash with the brand.

Reference-free metrics: key insights and choosing a strategy

▢ What the slide says

Application-specific quality
Reference-free qualities depend heavily on context and must be carefully defined for each use case.
Production reality
Creating ground truth in real time is usually impractical in production, so self-contained checks are vital for reliability.
Strategic selection
Choose the evaluation strategy based on available resources, specific quality needs and production constraints.

No one-size-fits-all approach: the method depends heavily on what you're evaluating.

💡 A practical way to combine both

Generalise theory

Example hypothesis: "Faster delivery increases customer trust scores by 20%." Not suitable for qualitative data!

💡 Why this doesn't work for agent failures

The delivery hypothesis works because you already know what to measure (delivery days, a trust score from 1 to 10) and can compare averages. With agent answers you don't know the categories in advance: you don't yet know that "ignores stock arriving tomorrow" is a failure type until you've read the answers. Starting from a hypothesis means you only find the problems you already guessed.

🍛 Analogy

Counting how many customers return within a week is quantitative: decide the question, then measure. Reading 50 complaint letters to discover why customers are unhappy is qualitative: the categories come out of the letters. Slide 23 is the method for the second kind.

Class activity: personal portfolio manager agent

▢ What the slide says

You're testing an AI agent that helps users manage their investments. Users ask vague or repetitive questions, so you need structured test queries that cover different behaviours.

  • Step 1, dimensions: asset type (stocks, mutual funds, crypto, bonds); goal (growth, income, risk management); action (buy, sell, hold, rebalance, compare).
  • Step 2, tuples: e.g. (Stocks, Growth, Buy), (Crypto, Risk Management, Sell), (Mutual Funds, Income, Compare).
  • Step 3, tuples into queries: "Which tech stocks should I buy for long-term growth?", "Should I sell my Bitcoin to reduce portfolio risk?", "Compare dividend-focused mutual funds that provide steady income."

Exercise: write code that uses dimensions → tuples → queries to generate 50 test queries for a portfolio manager agent, each sounding like something a real investor would ask.

⌨️ A solution

4 × 3 × 5 = 60 possible tuples; the code picks 50 different ones, then asks the model to phrase them 10 at a time (smaller batches give more varied wording) and saves everything to a CSV.

s24_portfolio_queries.pyNeeds OpenAI key
"""Slide 24 class activity: 50 test queries for a personal portfolio manager agent."""
import csv
import itertools
import json
import random

from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()
client = OpenAI()

dimensions = {
    "asset": ["Stocks", "Mutual Funds", "Crypto", "Bonds"],
    "goal": ["Growth", "Income", "Risk Management"],
    "action": ["Buy", "Sell", "Hold", "Rebalance", "Compare"],
}
all_tuples = list(itertools.product(*dimensions.values()))         # 4 × 3 × 5 = 60
random.seed(42)
tuples = random.sample(all_tuples, 50)                             # 50 different tuples

queries = []
for start in range(0, len(tuples), 10):                            # 10 tuples per call keeps answers varied
    batch = tuples[start:start + 10]
    prompt = ("Turn each (asset type, goal, action) tuple into one question a real retail investor in India "
              "would type to an investing assistant. Mix short and long, casual and precise. "
              'Reply as JSON: {"queries": [...]} in the same order.\n\n'
              + "\n".join(f"{i + 1}. {t}" for i, t in enumerate(batch)))
    reply = client.chat.completions.create(model="gpt-6-luna", reasoning_effort="none",
                                           response_format={"type": "json_object"},
                                           messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}])
    queries += json.loads(reply.choices[0].message.content)["queries"]

with open("portfolio_test_queries.csv", "w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    writer = csv.writer(f)
    writer.writerow(["asset", "goal", "action", "query"])
    writer.writerows([*t, q] for t, q in zip(tuples, queries))

print(f"Saved {len(queries)} queries to portfolio_test_queries.csv. First 8:\n")
for t, q in list(zip(tuples, queries))[:8]:
    print(f"{t[0]:<13}{t[1]:<16}{t[2]:<10} {q}")

⚠️ Things to discuss with learners

  • Not every tuple makes sense. (Bonds, Growth, Hold) is unusual. Keep a few anyway: real users ask odd things, and the agent should handle them gracefully.
  • Add the risky behaviours on purpose. A finance agent must not give definitive "buy this" advice (slide 20). Add a dimension such as tone (cautious / greedy / panicking) to test that.
  • Test queries need expected behaviour. For each query, write down what a good answer must do (e.g. "explains the risk, doesn't promise returns"). Then you can evaluate the agent, not just question it.

Glossary

Eval
A repeatable test of how well an agent (or one part of it) does its job.
Gold set / golden dataset
Test questions with agreed correct answers or labels.
Reference-based metric
Compares the output with a known correct answer.
Reference-free metric
Checks properties or rules of the output, with no answer key.
LLM-as-judge
Using a model, with a strict scoring prompt, to grade another model's answers.
Precision@k / Recall@k
Of the k passages retrieved, how many were useful / of the passages needed, how many were retrieved.
Groundedness
Every claim in the answer is supported by the retrieved text (the opposite of hallucination).
Tool match accuracy
How often the agent called the tool you expected.
Synthetic queries
Test questions generated from dimensions → tuples → natural wording.
Dimension / tuple
One way users vary (e.g. mood) / one combination of values (angry, refund, first order).
Error analysis
Reading failures, labelling them, and counting labels to find what to fix first.
Open / axial / selective coding
Label each note / group labels into categories / find the core explanation.
Annotator
A review screen where people mark answers pass/fail and write why.
Rubric
Written pass/fail criteria with examples.
Benevolent dictator
One trusted expert who makes the final call on labels.
Inter-annotator agreement (IAA)
How much different reviewers agree on the same items.
Chance agreement
Agreement you'd expect from the reviewers' label habits alone.
Cohen's kappa
(observed − chance) ÷ (1 − chance): agreement beyond luck, from below 0 up to 1.

Check yourself (click to reveal)

1. Your prompt says "Answer the question." Answers are correct but miss edge cases. Which gulf is this?

The gulf of specification: what you want ("handle the hidden assumption") isn't written in the prompt. Version 2 of the prompt in the code fixes exactly this.

2. Why can't you test an agent with assert agent(q) == expected_text?

The same input gives different wording each time, and many different answers can be correct. Use keyword checks, execution checks, rules or an LLM judge with a rubric instead.

3. A question needs passages [2, 3]. You retrieve [3, 9, 2]. What are Precision@3 and Recall@3?

Precision@3 = 2/3 (two of the three retrieved were useful). Recall@3 = 2/2 = 1.0 (both needed passages were retrieved).

4. "Generate 50 test questions about refunds" gives near-duplicates. What's the fix?

Define dimensions (issue, mood, order context), build tuples in code, then ask the model only to phrase each tuple. Keep the two steps separate.

5. Two reviewers agree 85% of the time. Both mark 90% of answers "pass". Is that good agreement?

No. Chance agreement = 0.9 × 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.82. Kappa = (0.85 − 0.82) / (1 − 0.82) ≈ 0.17: only slight agreement beyond luck.

6. Is "the answer contains no investment advice" a reference-based or reference-free check?

Reference-free: it checks a property of the output, with no correct answer to compare against.

7. After error analysis, 6 of 9 failures are "misses the hidden edge case" and 1 is "too long". What do you change first, and how do you know it worked?

Change the prompt to make the advisor check the question's hidden assumption, then rerun the same eval set and compare Completeness (and the other metrics, to be sure nothing got worse).
