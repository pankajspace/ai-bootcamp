Send the prescriptive Part 6 prompt

The goal of Part 6 is to model a real-world way of triggering workflows. Until now everything in our app happened because a user clicked something. But an auction ending is different: nobody clicks “end”. The clock simply passes endsAt. Some piece of the backend has to notice that on its own, declare the auction closed, work out who won, and tell the people who care. In guide 19 I explained vague vs prescriptive prompts. In guide 17 I was fairly open and let Codex propose the design. Here I am deliberately prescriptive, because I already know the architecture I want, and it is the architecture most real companies use for this kind of job. Three concepts before you read the prompt:

📘 Concept: polling worker

A worker is a backend program that is not a web server. Nobody sends it requests; it runs in the background and does jobs. A polling worker wakes up on a timer, every second in our case, and asks the database one question: “are there any auctions whose endsAt is in the past and that I have not closed yet?” If yes, it closes them and records the winner. If no, it goes back to sleep. Polling is the simplest way to react to time passing. It is a bit wasteful (most checks find nothing), but it is easy to understand, easy to test, and it keeps working after a crash because it just asks again on the next tick.

📘 Concept: message queue, RabbitMQ, producer, consumer, ack, redelivery

A message queue is a post box between two programs. One program, the producer, drops a message in (“auction 42 closed, winner is Maya”) and moves on; it does not wait for anyone to read it. Another program, the consumer, picks messages out of the box and acts on them. The two never talk directly, so you can restart, slow down or scale either one without the other noticing. RabbitMQ is a popular, battle-tested message queue server; we run it in Docker like Postgres. When a consumer finishes handling a message, it sends an ack (acknowledgement) and RabbitMQ deletes the message. If the consumer crashes before acking, RabbitMQ assumes the work was not done and hands the same message to a consumer again. That is redelivery, and it is why queues promise at-least-once delivery rather than exactly-once. It also means your consumer must cope with seeing the same message twice; more on that in step 4.

📘 Concept: notification worker → WebSocket popup

Our second worker is the notification worker. It is the consumer of the queue: it waits for “auction closed” messages. For each one it decides who should be told (the winner, the seller) and then pushes a message to those people's browsers over the WebSocket channel we built in Part 4. The browser receives it and shows a popup. So the full chain is: clock passes → close worker notices → close worker writes to Postgres and produces a message → RabbitMQ → notification worker consumes it → Socket.IO (via the Redis backplane) → browser popup. Five hops, each one small and testable on its own. That is the whole point of splitting it up.

Answer round 1 in the main chat

Back in the main chat (“Grill auction notifications design”). Codex asks its own questions, and the wording and order will differ from mine, so match by topic, not by number. Here is what my answers decided and why:

  1. What “auction ended” means → take recommend: endsAt is the business cutoff, and a worker records a durable close afterwards. In other words, the clock decides when bidding stops, but the database row only says “closed” once the worker has processed it.
  2. Who the winner is → take recommend: the highest accepted bid in PostgreSQL, never anything the browser or the socket layer believes. The database is the single source of truth.
  3. Zero bids → take recommend: still close it and publish AuctionClosed with no winner. The seller still needs to know it ended.
  4. Exactly-once experience → RabbitMQ can redeliver, so Codex asked whether a duplicate popup is acceptable. My answer is a loud no: we must handle idempotency.
  5. Notify offline winners later → no. Live only. Storing notifications for later is a whole feature by itself, and not today's.
  6. Where the popup appears → a modal over the whole screen, anywhere in the app, not only on the auction page.
  7. Who receives it → the winner and the auction owner (seller), so the seller sees that their auction ended.
  8. Popup behaviour → requires dismissal. It should not fade away on its own. Make it really annoying, in a good way.
  9. Timing → take recommend: within about 5 seconds of endsAt.

📘 Concept: idempotent consumer

Idempotent means “doing it twice has the same result as doing it once”. Pressing a lift button twice does not call two lifts. Because RabbitMQ delivers at-least-once (see step 1), the notification worker will occasionally receive the same “auction 42 closed” message twice: after a network blip, or if the worker crashed just before sending its ack. An idempotent consumer notices “I already handled auction 42” and quietly skips the duplicate, instead of showing the winner two popups. The usual way is to give every message a unique id and record handled ids in the database; before acting, check the record. This one habit separates toy queue code from production queue code.

📘 Concept: modal

A modal is a popup window that takes over the page: the rest of the screen dims, and you cannot click anything behind it until you deal with the modal. “Modal” means it changes the page's mode from normal to “answer this first”. Compare it to a toast, the small notice that appears in a corner and disappears by itself. We want a modal here because a won auction is important: the user must see it and must close it deliberately. Answers 6 and 8 together describe exactly that.

Round 2 is about reliability and about which program owns which job. What my answers decided:

  • Q11 End-to-end idempotency → take recommend, with the outbox pattern (explained below).
  • Q12 Connected sessions and identity → take recommend: a user may have three tabs open, so send to every connected session of each recipient.
  • Q13 Modal copy by outcome → take recommend: different text for the winner, for a seller whose auction had a winner, and for a seller whose auction had no bids.
  • Q14 Several auctions closing together → take recommend: queue the modals and show one at a time, never stack five popups on top of each other.

📘 Concept: transactional outbox

Here is the problem. The close worker must do two things: save “auction 42 is closed, winner Maya” in Postgres, and publish a message to RabbitMQ. Those are two different systems, and you cannot wrap both in one database transaction. If the worker saves to Postgres and then crashes before publishing, the auction is closed but nobody is ever told. If it publishes first and then the save fails, people are told about a close that did not happen. The transactional outbox fixes this with a trick: instead of publishing directly, the worker writes the message into an outbox table in the same transaction as the close. Either both rows land or neither does. A separate small loop (the publisher) then reads unpublished outbox rows, sends them to RabbitMQ, and marks them published only once RabbitMQ confirms it has them. If anything crashes in between, the row is still sitting in the outbox and gets sent on the next loop. Nothing is lost; at worst, something is sent twice, which is exactly why the consumer must be idempotent.

📘 Concept: ADR (architecture decision record)

An ADR is a short text file, usually half a page, that records one lasting architectural choice: what we decided, why, what the alternatives were, and what we knowingly gave up. They live in the repo (here under docs/adr/) and are numbered in order, so a year later a new engineer can read 0001, 0002, 0003 and understand how the system came to look the way it does. The habit matters even more with coding agents: a future Codex session will read these files and respect the decision, instead of “helpfully” replacing the outbox with direct publishing because it looked simpler. Think of an ADR as a note to your future self and to your future agent.

Key takeaways

  • Prompts range from vague to prescriptive. When you already know the architecture you want, say so in plain sentences. A vague prompt lets the model choose, and hidden context like AGENTS.md may end up choosing for it.
  • Two workers, one queue. A polling close worker turns “the clock passed” into a saved fact in Postgres. RabbitMQ carries the event. A notification worker turns the event into a WebSocket popup. Each hop is small and testable alone.
  • Message queues are at-least-once. A consumer crash before ack means redelivery. Design idempotency at every boundary: one close per auction, one outbox event, one delivery per recipient, one modal per notification.
  • Transactional outbox: write the state change and the event in the same database transaction, then publish from the outbox table. Nothing gets lost between Postgres and RabbitMQ.
  • Record decisions in ADRs. A half-page note today saves a future human, and a future agent, from undoing the decision by accident.
  • Keep scope tight. Dead-letter queues and retry backoff are real production concerns. “Don't build any of that today” keeps the slice small enough to finish and review.
